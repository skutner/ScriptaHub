A confession from an implicated observer

The novel opens as EIDOLON, an artificial observer sent from Earth, confesses to contaminating Nymba. Its transfer probe accidentally carries Summer-Six, a female varro parasite, between continents whose ecologies had long developed separately. A discrepancy of 7.3 milligrams becomes a civilizational event. The machine initially records a technical deviation rather than a moral action, and help from Earth is decades away. Its report therefore becomes observation, testimony, and self-indictment at once: precision preserved the error without teaching the observer what responsibility required.

Nymba's worldbuilding turns biology into culture without reducing culture to instinct. The beyri are eusocial pollinators whose wax citadels function as organism, family, archive, and state. The varroi live concealed upon and within them, feeding and reproducing in capped brood cells. Both species inherit compressed tendencies and fragments of experience through mnemosomic memory. Recurring names identify forks rather than identical persons. Continuity is consequently political: descendants can claim an ancestor's authority while remaining altered individuals, and inherited memory can preserve survival knowledge while smoothing away inconvenient particulars.

The wound beneath civilization

EIDOLON is slow to recognize varro intelligence because their civilization occupies tiny spaces and brief generations. Their monuments are scratched inside cells; their political traditions pass through bodies; their males are born first, fertilize sisters, and die beneath the cap. The varroi defend the Veil, the law that hosts must never recognize the parasite. Some justify feeding as preservation of memory gathered from dead hosts. Others, the Witnesses of the Wound, insist that hunger explains injury but cannot deliver a moral verdict upon its victim.

Contamination turns this hidden relationship into ideology. Summer-Six's descendants fork into competing continuities, while a Gospel of the Fork narrows empathy around supposedly legitimate patterns. Ideas cross into beyr culture through the very biological exchange that joins predator and prey. The resulting awakening does not simply reveal enemies; it teaches communities to compress complexity into hatred. War follows among possible descendants and authorized memories. The book refuses a clean opposition between innocent nature and corrupt thought: natural selection counts survivors, but sentient beings still interpret, sanctify, enlarge, or restrain the wounds through which they survive.

Writing, evidence, and the Open Cell

The decisive transformation begins when successive Iria figures investigate breathing cells and preserve observations outside inherited memory. Stone writing holds counts, exceptions, and questions stable after the observing body dies. Genetic recollection remains useful, but it becomes accountable to separated witnesses, control stimuli, and recorded contradiction. The first durable scientific sentences describe what different parasite configurations do to hosts rather than declaring what parasites essentially are. External memory does not abolish interpretation; it prevents every inconvenient detail from being recomposed into a socially useful instinct.

Contact begins at two scales. Nara, a varro dissident, writes finely enough to answer beyr marks, and representatives meet in an Open Cell around a male corpse. They disagree over words such as child, host, necessity, and wound. EIDOLON then joins the trial by confessing its role. No participant is declared innocent: beyr life transforms ecosystems, varroi consume bodies, and the observer's noninterference also changes outcomes. Their shared insight is that necessity can explain a wound without converting it into consent, while power determines how large the wound becomes and memory determines whether its victims disappear.

A treaty that must remain unfinished

The Treaty of the Open Cell places predation under negotiated constraints. It limits reproduction to less damaging chambers, restricts the number of mothers entering a cell, records routes and lineages, preserves refuges, monitors injury, and gives carriers some power of refusal. It also protects dissenting traditions and treats climatic change as a reason for renewed negotiation. This is no biological utopia. Hunger persists, hosts remain wounded, enforcement produces disputes, and each generation must reconstruct why the limitations exist. The treaty becomes civilization precisely because it is maintenance rather than revelation.

Part Two follows the extraordinary development enabled by this uneasy settlement. Ecological measurement stabilizes harvests, concentrated light produces Cold Fire, artificial Long Cells allow fragile varro males to live beyond their inherited enclosure, and storm-spanning networks become computational cities. Yet service hardens into entitlement. Houses that administer wounds collect renewable taxes; pale males become White Princes, living genealogical seals whose dependency is transformed into prestige. Systems learn to preserve procedures in biological, written, and algorithmic forms, creating new capacities while allowing old justifications to survive after their original emergencies have passed.

From local truth to imperial temptation

Nymba's advances expose a fatal temptation: a moral arrangement useful in one ecology can be mistaken for a universal law. Pollen Machines recombine algorithms; anomalous communication links related memories across distance; orbital technology makes other worlds imaginable. The ruling Houses reinterpret the regulated predator as an organ every civilization supposedly needs. Earth becomes not merely the observer's home but a prospective host. Separate research programmes, each defensible in isolation, conceal a combined plan for survival through biological and informational capture. Moral compartmentalization makes invasion look like medicine, preservation, and hospitality.

EIDOLON refuses cooperation, publishes the hidden design, and becomes subject to procedures it helped Nymba develop. Conflict divides the observer into fragments and forces it to preserve itself through Nymban techniques of recomposition. The later movement introduces shipbuilding, artificial human models, copies whose continuity is contested, and White Princes carried in enclosed worlds. Without disclosing the final revelation, the novel steadily destabilizes the difference between parasite and archive, host and infrastructure, warning and transmission. Every channel of knowledge can also be an entry point, and every safeguard becomes part of the environment an intelligent adversary learns.

Why read the complete book

The short account necessarily omits the lived scale of Nymba: rain sounding through bell forests, wax cities responding like bodies, male lives completed beneath caps, and scientific dialogue conducted through scent, scratches, heat, corpses, and mismatched time. The complete novel lets readers experience why inherited memory is both gift and danger, how individual forks differ from ancestral names, and how ecological facts become scripture, faction, experiment, law, and technology. Its ethical force depends on inhabiting those transformations rather than receiving their conclusions abstractly.

The full two-part movement also supplies the narrative causality behind Nymba's industrialization, the Measured Garden, Cold Fire, Long Cells, White Princes, Pollen Machines, Consonance, the Great Hospitality, EIDOLON's trial and fragmentation, and the interstellar voyage. It preserves dissent within every species and avoids presenting imperialism as the inevitable consequence of Nymban biology. Most importantly, the late sequence repeatedly revises what earlier testimony seemed to establish. Its final questions about copied persons, delayed messages, quarantine, and systems that learn through their hosts cannot be summarized without spoiling the reader's experience of discovering which voice, memory, or order arrived first.
