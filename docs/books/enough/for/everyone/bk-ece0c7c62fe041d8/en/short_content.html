1. The Book in Ten Minutes: Conclusions Before the Demonstration

1.1 The Verdict and the Material Inventory

With reasonable probability, humanity has enough energy, matter, water, and biological capacity for no human being to remain constrained by lack of food, decent housing, basic medical treatment, education, connectivity, or a reasonable degree of mobility. This conclusion assumes neither infinite resources nor miraculous technology. It assumes the expansion of technologies already known, several plausible advances in automation, and an institutional reorganization that converts productive capacity into effective access. In the restricted sense used here, a basic post-scarcity era is physically possible within this century.

Absolute post-scarcity, however, is impossible. A person's time, other people's attention, highly desired places, intact ecosystems, political power, status, unique objects, irreversible experiences, and consent-based relationships cannot be multiplied on demand. Even if energy, intelligence, and labor become extremely cheap, not everyone can be first, most admired, the decision-maker, or the partner of the same person. Economics does not disappear; it shifts from calories, kilowatts, and labor hours toward time, authenticity, risk, social position, and institutional control.

The right question is therefore not whether we can eliminate every form of scarcity, but whether we can detach survival and dignity from competition for resources. My answer is yes. We can construct a floor below which no one falls without pretending that every desire will be satisfied or every outcome made equal. Realistic abundance means universal sufficiency, redundancy, and the freedom to refuse degrading work; it does not mean unlimited consumption or the homogenization of lives.

The book's most important result is that the near-term limit does not appear to be the planet's raw inventory. The limits are the speed at which we open mines and refineries, build grids and housing, train people, automate processes, protect ecosystems, and change rules of ownership. These processes are slow, conflict-ridden, and unevenly distributed. A society can have cheap energy and poor citizens; enough food and hunger; productive robots and people with little bargaining power. Post-scarcity is not an automatic outcome of technology but a political achievement enabled by technology.

Energy is the most favorable part of the balance sheet. The world energy system today operates at roughly twenty terawatts, while Earth intercepts approximately 174,000 terawatts of solar radiation. We cannot and should not capture more than a tiny fraction of that flow. Even a fifty- or one-hundred-terawatt civilization would use only a very small share of incident solar energy. The real problem is industrial: panels, turbines, power plants, grids, transformers, storage, materials, permitting, maintenance, and the cost of capital. The rapid growth of renewables shows that supply can be multiplied within a generation, but availability at every hour and in every region requires a plural system, not a single technology.

Nor is matter, in general, close to geological exhaustion. Iron, aluminum, silicon, and limestone are abundant, although extracting and processing them consumes energy, degrades ecosystems, and produces emissions. For copper, lithium, graphite, nickel, and other critical materials, the near-term danger is a capacity shortage: mining and refining projects are not expanding fast enough, and production is geographically concentrated. Recycling, design for long life, substitution, and standardization can reduce pressure, but they cannot fully replace extraction while global infrastructure is still expanding. We are not “running out of atoms”; for years or decades, we may run short of enough mines, refineries, permits, and logistics chains.

Food is already produced at a scale that could feed everyone, but not necessarily in the form, place, and at the price each person needs. Contemporary hunger coexists with enormous losses and waste, the use of grain for animals and fuels, conflict, and populations unable to afford a healthy diet. Higher yields, lower losses, plant breeding, efficient irrigation, precision agriculture, and a larger share of lower-impact proteins create substantial room for improvement. Vertical farming is useful for certain perishable crops and in certain cities, but it is not an energetically rational solution for staple grains. Land, soil, and biodiversity remain more important constraints than the number of floors in a farm.

Water is globally abundant and locally scarce. Urban water can be desalinated and recycled at a manageable energy cost in an expanded electric economy. The water required for food is of a different order of magnitude and must come mainly from rainfall, healthy soils, protected watersheds, and efficient irrigation. The conclusion is asymmetric: we can guarantee drinking water almost anywhere, but we cannot turn every desert into a vast agricultural region without very high ecological and energy costs. Post-scarcity requires adapting production to geography, not abolishing geography.

Housing is a mixed case. Materials and construction labor can become much cheaper, while well-located land, urban infrastructure, and permits can keep prices high. An economy with robots capable of erecting buildings quickly can still have a housing crisis if cities block supply or land rents absorb the productivity gain. In many domains, the final problem will not be manufacturing the object but the right to place it and access to the surrounding system.

1.2 The Scarcity That Remains and the Realistic Timeline

The first hard scarcity is lived time. Automation can free hours, and medicine can extend healthy life, but every human remains a sequential being. No one can read every book, live in every city, pursue every career, or sustain an unlimited number of deep relationships. As AI produces more options, the value of filtering and choosing rises. An abundance of answers creates a scarcity of reasons to trust any particular answer.

The second category consists of ecological and geographic goods. A mature forest, a reef, a species, a stable climate, or a historic city center is not merely an aggregate of matter. Such things can be protected, partly restored, or imitated, but they have a history and a location that cannot be copied without loss. Cheap energy can make more places comfortable and reduce pressure on nature; it can also accelerate extraction, transport, and destruction. The biosphere is not merely a supplier of inputs but a system with thresholds and irreversibilities.

The third category is relational. Trust, intimacy, love, admiration, and consent cannot be distributed like electricity. Other people's bodies and relationships are not resources to which material equality creates a right of access. A richer society can remove economic coercion from many relationships and make consent more genuine; it cannot guarantee everyone the partner, attention, or recognition they desire. Status competition may become more visible precisely here, because money can no longer purchase other people's dependence as easily.

Power is scarce in an even more direct sense. The capacity to decide for others is relational: not everyone can control the same institution. Technology can distribute tools, but infrastructures of energy, chips, models, and robots can create unprecedented centers of power. A materially abundant world can be democratic or authoritarian. Comfort is not the same as freedom, and free services can be offered in exchange for surveillance and conformity.

Status and positional goods will almost certainly survive. Not everyone can be in the top one percent, at the exclusive table, or the author of the first discovery. People compare not only whether they have enough but also their position relative to others. Realistic politics cannot eliminate every difference in prestige; it can prevent prestige from becoming a right to dominate, a monopoly over institutions, or control over other people's material lives. This is the difference between a society with functional hierarchies and one based on subordination.

There is no single date on which the world “enters” post-scarcity. Digital intelligence becomes cheaper faster than physical infrastructure; new energy capacity is built faster than cities; rich economies may universalize services decades before fragile regions. The transition will be a sequence of thresholds: first an abundance of translation, programming, design, and analysis; then automation of factories, warehouses, and transport; later general robotics in open environments; and latest of all, the reorganization of ownership and public services.

The book's central scenario places the possibility of a basic post-scarcity threshold in some rich economies between 2040 and 2060. A global version becomes plausible between 2070 and 2100. For 2050, I subjectively estimate a 35–55% probability that several high-income states will reach the threshold and a 10–20% probability that it will be reached worldwide. For 2070, the ranges rise to 65–85% in rich economies and 35–60% globally. By 2100, worldwide attainment is physically plausible but remains conditional on avoiding systemic wars, ecological collapses, and extreme concentration of infrastructure.

These percentages are not the output of a predictive model deserving decimal precision. They are prospective judgments intended to make the assumptions falsifiable. If electricity grids, mining, robotics, and housing accelerate faster than I assume here, the timeline should be shortened. If robotic safety stagnates, geopolitical tensions fragment trade, or productivity gains are captured by rents, the timeline should be extended. The book offers a map to update, not an oracle.

The personal robot will probably emerge in stages and will resemble the automobile economically more than the phone. The first useful systems will be expensive, imperfect, and dependent on servicing, remote supervision, or simplified environments. Access through fleets, subscriptions, buildings, care services, and rentals will be more widespread than individual ownership. In the baseline scenario, fewer than 1% of the world's households have a dedicated general-purpose robot in 2030; 1–4% in 2035; 8–20% in 2045; and 25–50% in 2055. In rich economies, access could reach 5–15% in 2035, 25–55% in 2045, and 60–85% in 2055.

The decisive economic criterion is not the sticker price but the annual cost per hour of safe, useful work. A cheap robot that breaks down, requires a human operator, and cannot work around children is expensive. A more costly robot that operates for thousands of hours, lowers the cost of care, and can be upgraded is affordable as a service. Mass diffusion will begin when reliability, maintenance, and legal liability mature, not when the first spectacular demo appears.

1.3 The Final Obstacle: Institutions and Human Nature

Humanity does not appear programmed for a single social form. We are capable of extensive cooperation, sharing, reciprocity, and sanctioning dominators, but also of status competition, coalition-building, exploitation, and obedience. Hunter-gatherer societies described as egalitarian were not devoid of differences in age, gender, reputation, skill, or reproductive success. Their egalitarianism was often an active practice: mobility, compulsory sharing, consensus decision-making, ridicule of braggarts, and the ability to leave a leader. Agriculture, storage, inheritable land, large domesticated animals, and warfare made wealth more accumulable and domination easier to institutionalize (Borgerhoff Mulder et al., 2009; Kohler et al., 2017; Stibbard-Hawkes and von Rueden, 2025).

This history partly supports the observation that many people would rather exchange places with the dominator than abolish domination. Aversion to inequality is often stronger when a person loses than when that person gains. Stable systems offer those at the bottom the hope of mobility, identification with the dominant group, moral explanations of hierarchy, and small positions from which they can dominate someone even more vulnerable. The literature on system justification sometimes finds implicit preferences for advantaged groups among members of disadvantaged groups, but explicit findings are far more heterogeneous and include solidarity, resistance, and in-group preference (Essien et al., 2021). There is no law saying that the oppressed love oppression; there are simply enough mechanisms through which they can reproduce it.

Genetic variation is real, including for traits related to personality and orientation toward hierarchy, but it does not justify fatalism. Twin studies measure variation within a population in a particular environment; they do not discover a “gene for exploitation” and do not show that institutions are irrelevant. A population may contain people strongly oriented toward dominance, people oriented toward prestige and contribution, conflict-averse people, and many intermediate combinations. Good institutions do not assume that everyone becomes moral; they limit the capacity of any temperament to convert local advantage into hereditary and uncontrollable power.

The disappearance of economic coercion does not imply the disappearance of motivation. Research on autonomous motivation shows that people pursue competence, autonomy, and relatedness, not only monetary reward. Guaranteed-income experiments have produced modest reductions in working time, not a general withdrawal from activity; in the U.S. experiment analyzed by Vivalt and colleagues, the reduction was roughly one to two hours per week (Vivalt et al., 2024). At the same time, involuntary unemployment harms mental health, while chronic boredom is associated with aggression and the search for destructive stimulation. An abundant society must provide structure, challenges, roles, belonging, and paths to prestige that do not depend on the threat of hunger.

The political verdict is harsher than the physical one. We can produce enough without providing enough. We can automate labor and concentrate ownership. We can guarantee goods while conditioning freedom. The most robust architecture would combine a universal floor of services, distributed ownership of productive infrastructure, genuine competition, limits on the conversion of wealth into political power, protection of privacy, and multiple paths to prestige through creation, knowledge, care, courage, and public service. Post-scarcity need not abolish ambition. It must make ambition compatible with the freedom of others.

This distinction also changes how the timeline should be read. There will be no morning on which the world officially wakes up “post-scarcity.” Some services will cross the threshold earlier: information, basic education, and part of diagnosis may become nearly free before housing, care, and mobility do. Some regions will gain abundant energy but remain constrained by water, administration, or conflict. Others will have strong distributive institutions but aging infrastructure. The transition will look like a map of islands of sufficiency, corridors of access, and abandoned zones, not a clean global line.

The human obstacle is not only the greed of an elite. It is also the collective difficulty of accepting that essential goods no longer need to be used as instruments of discipline. Some people will believe that security without compulsory work weakens character; others will defend privileges as evidence of merit; a minority will lose not comfort but the power to purchase obedience. The

book does not assume these reactions will disappear. Its verdict is more modest: competitive and

hierarchical predispositions can be channeled above a universal material floor, and institutions can prevent success from becoming an unlimited right over other people's lives.

There is also a cultural difficulty: modern societies have tied virtue to imposed effort and personal worth to the scarcity of one's position. If basic goods become easy to access, some old signals of merit will lose value. The reaction may be creative—new forms of excellence may emerge—or defensive, through the invention of new barriers, credentials, licenses, exclusivities, and ranks. The transition toward sufficiency therefore requires not only cheaper production but also a culture capable of granting prestige for contribution, competence, care, and discovery without needing other people's poverty as a backdrop.
