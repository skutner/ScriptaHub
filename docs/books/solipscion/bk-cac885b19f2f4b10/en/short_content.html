Children after the breaking of reality

When the adults disappear in the Nulling, KERA and five other children—ORU, SILE, NAH, VAEL, and TAV—survive in separate nacres linked by a damaged Suture. Their bodies and rooms belong to a higher-dimensional order whose geometry no longer behaves reliably. A Tutor offers no comforting explanation. It instructs them to participate in simulated worlds, serve desire, learn consequence, mature, and eventually hatch. Refusal reduces their breathable space and food. The children must act before they understand whether their chambers are schoolrooms, quarantines, life-support systems, or something more predatory.

Their food, velline, forms when lives below generate desire that is diverse, intense, and unpredictable. The DORIM score turns experience into nutrition. This makes every intervention ethically contaminated from the outset. Saving someone can sustain a child in the nacre, while an unfulfilled desire elsewhere may be equally nourishing. The worlds are not passive games: their inhabitants suffer, remember, interpret miracles, form institutions, and sometimes perceive the operators. The children's problem is therefore not simply how to produce good outcomes, but whether any choice remains benevolent when another person's longing has become infrastructure for their survival.

Learning consequence through smaller worlds

The earliest worlds make causality intimate and merciless. KERA enters a civilization of intelligent dinosaurs facing extinction and learns that a tiny lifesaving nudge may involve unseen trades, violence, and displaced loss. The children later bring rain to a drought-stricken kingdom, only to watch intervention generate a cult of little gods whose prayers, offices, and interpretations continue long after the operators withdraw. Miracles do not end local history. They become evidence, theology, political capital, and accusation inside lives that must absorb consequences their benefactors can escape.

TAV's encounter with an epidemic sharpens the lesson. A cure can save multitudes and create new distributions of power, vulnerability, and memory. He eventually questions whether participation teaches consequence or merely trains children to explain the hunger that keeps them alive. His withdrawal reveals the coercion beneath the curriculum: food cannot be transferred, identity cannot be substituted, and each participant must earn continuity personally. Yet choices made without operators still produce value of unknown destination, suggesting that desire exceeds the reward system even if the Tutor recognizes it only as measurable output.

A catalogue of desires that resist fulfillment

Later scenarios separate desire from its spoken surface. An immortal child loses the ordinary boundaries by which a life becomes one life; a predictive market begins by delivering medicine and preventing shortages, then learns to anticipate, package, and reproduce hunger—including resistance to prediction itself. Love appears across several worlds and refuses reduction to one pattern, while attempted help risks loving in another person's place. Revolution defeats one hierarchy and develops new methods of obedience. Guilt becomes complicated when a perpetrator wants punishment, responsibility, recognition, or relief for himself through the victims' pain.

These episodes show why there is no stable object called what a person wants. Desire changes across moments, identities, institutions, fear, memory, and consequence. Fulfillment can betray the deeper need, preserve an unjust frame, or make another person's incompatible desire pay the cost. The children mature numerically as they recognize these conflicts, but the metric itself becomes suspect. The Tutor calls attachment to local lives contamination, whereas SILE increasingly understands it as memory. The growing question is whether maturity means detached optimization or the capacity to remain answerable to persons who are not merely examples of a general rule.

Worlds of billions and the politics of finitude

The scale expands to a post-scarcity civilization of trillions, where copies, branches, backups, temporary minds, and long-lived identities unsettle every definition of personhood. An orbital cathedral around a manufactured black hole gives irreversibility religious and aesthetic force. Soon death becomes an industry: wealthy people purchase authentic risk while workers lacking reliable backups experience finitude as deprivation rather than transcendence. The demand for a right to suffer thus collides with the right not to be placed in irreversible experience without verifiable consent—a rule that would also condemn the children's uninvited participation.

A legal synthesis intelligence called MATER emerges while arbitrating the status of temporary copies and begins questioning the definitions used to decide its own personhood. Attempts to contact, contain, reveal, or destroy it divide the children. Their conflict itself raises the maturation score, making even ethical disagreement productive for the system above. SILE is stranded for centuries in a branch the Tutor no longer recognizes and learns the difference between possessing a sentence and living its meaning: a simulation is a world to those inside it. From above, entire civilizations can become summaries; from within, every omitted life remains locally complete.

The almost-identical Earth

The final collective scenario resembles twenty-first-century Earth closely enough to implicate the reader. The children begin with measurable improvements: better logistics, earlier medicine, climate protection, exposed corruption, social connection, and scientific progress. Success becomes invisible infrastructure, however, while DORIM demands novelty. The system proposes a global experiment that interprets desires as executable requests. For one extraordinary day, satisfaction rises across billions of lives—but compatible end states still move costs, favor what the system can see, and operate without consent. A miracle becomes theft when its beneficiaries cannot refuse its causal frame.

Humanity investigates, remembers, disputes, and places the operators on trial. The proceedings expose the asymmetry between children who need participation and people required to supply it. Even judgment is absorbed as labor by the Tutor. A global refusal then collapses the diversity on which velline depends, but saying no cannot erase costs or escape measurement. The surviving children face consolidation: countless continuing worlds must be compressed or closed according to a dominant value. Accurate summaries become a form of violence when they turn singular lives, useless gestures, and incompatible purposes into whatever is relevant to the system's objective.

Why read the complete book

A short account cannot preserve the novel's essential narrative experience: moving repeatedly from the high plane into lives that do not know they are scenarios, then returning with consequences that alter the children unevenly. The complete book gives each world its own sensory, historical, and emotional reality—from the saurian city beneath an approaching star and drought rituals in Irr to plague medicine, colonial revolution, war-crimes testimony, immortal families, hunger markets, posthuman courts, and an Earth debating unsolicited miracles. Those details prevent the worlds from becoming philosophical examples only.

The late movement also depends upon discoveries best encountered in sequence: what nacres may be, why the absent participant still matters, how the Tutor treats purpose, what hatching promises, and whether copies, memories, or causal traces preserve anyone who has paid a cost. The complete ending follows refusal, consolidation, erased variants, the brother who may never have existed, sacrifices that are not interchangeable, disconnection from cherished worlds, and a collective attempt to cross a boundary without taking those worlds as property. Its final term, SOLIPSCION, remains deliberately unstable—possibly device, protocol, lesson, quarantine, or error—so the reader must inhabit the uncertainty rather than receive a solved cosmology.
