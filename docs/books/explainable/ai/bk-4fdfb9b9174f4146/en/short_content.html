1.1 When accuracy stops being enough

Imagine two teams deploying the same risk model. The first celebrates a small increase in AUROC and publishes a dashboard with a SHAP plot. The second discovers, during a complaint investigation, that nobody can answer a basic question: did the model reject this person because of information that should have been irrelevant, because of a legitimate but surprising interaction, or because the input was outside the region where the model had been validated? Both teams possess the same predictive model. Only one has treated explanation as an engineering requirement. This distinction is the practical beginning of XAI.

Accuracy stops being enough whenever a prediction becomes a decision, evidence for a decision, or a component inside a longer process. In debugging, an engineer needs an explanation that is sensitive to the trained model rather than merely visually plausible. In medicine, a clinician may need to know whether a finding is consistent with domain knowledge, whether the system is uncertain, and whether a recommendation survives a plausible change in measurement conditions.

In an automated agent, an operator may need to reconstruct which retrieved document triggered a tool call and whether a different source would have changed the action. Each setting changes the explanatory target.

A useful mental model is to treat prediction and explanation as two different interfaces. The prediction interface answers a task question: class, score, text continuation, ranking, action. The explanation interface answers a question about the system: dependence, contrast, mechanism, provenance, uncertainty, or policy compliance. The second interface therefore cannot be specified by saying only “use SHAP” or “show the reasoning.” It needs requirements just as an API does. Haufe and colleagues' 2026 call for formalization is important because it gives theoretical language to a problem engineers repeatedly encounter: a method may return numbers that are mathematically well defined while the user interprets those numbers as answers to a different question.

The story of modern XAI can be read as a sequence of attempts to close this gap. Early post-hoc methods made black boxes locally inspectable. Later work showed that explanations could remain plausible under model randomization, could be manipulated adversarially, or could collapse under small changes in baseline and background distribution. Counterfactual and recourse research exposed the difference between changing a model input and changing a person's feasible options. Human-centered work showed that a technically faithful explanation can still create the wrong mental model. Mechanistic interpretability now goes deeper, asking whether internal computations can be identified and causally tested. The field is not abandoning older tools; it is learning to place each tool inside a more explicit evidential hierarchy.

Suppose a credit model rejects an application. A data scientist may ask which input variables most influenced the score. A regulator may ask whether the decision depended on a prohibited attribute or a proxy. The applicant may ask what could realistically be changed to obtain a different outcome. A model developer may ask whether the classifier learned a shortcut. A security engineer may ask whether the model can be manipulated into producing a benign-looking explanation while retaining undesirable behavior. These are not different phrasings of one question. They request different objects, require different evidence, and fail in different ways.

The purpose of this table is to prevent the word explanation from collapsing distinct engineering tasks into one interface. The first column names the operational question; the second states the kind of evidence the system must eventually support.

DebuggingEvidence that identifies model behavior or failure conditions and reacts when the trained model changes.

RecourseEvidence that links a changed decision to feasible interventions in the world, not only editable inputs.

The table also shows why a single visual explanation cannot satisfy every stakeholder. A debugging artifact may be technically excellent and still be useless for recourse or audit because those tasks require different assumptions and downstream evidence.

This is the first source of confusion in XAI. The word explanation is used for feature importance, local sensitivity, global approximation, counterfactual change, causal effect, training-data influence, concept dependence, example similarity, attention patterns, generated rationales, and hypotheses about internal mechanisms. A method can be correct for one of these meanings and misleading for another. Treating the outputs as interchangeable is therefore a semantic error before it becomes a statistical one.

The problem-first view is now receiving explicit theoretical support. Haufe and colleagues argue that much of XAI developed algorithm-first: researchers created attribution techniques and later attached broad explanatory interpretations to their outputs. Their 2026 perspective calls instead for formally defining the explanatory problem, specifying correctness criteria, and validating against controlled or synthetic ground truth. The engineering translation is simple: the API of an explainer should include its semantics, not only its function signature.

Terminology remains inconsistent across the literature, so this book adopts an operational convention. Interpretability refers to properties that make the predictive mechanism or representation understandable enough to inspect directly. Explainability is broader and includes post-hoc procedures that interrogate or approximate systems whose native computation is not readily understandable. Transparency refers to the amount and kind of information exposed about the model, its data, its training process, and its operation. These terms overlap, but keeping them distinct prevents several common mistakes.

A sparse linear model can be intrinsically interpretable when its variables are meaningful and the number of interactions remains manageable. A gradient-boosted tree ensemble may be opaque as a whole but admit high-quality local or global analyses. A large language model can be highly transparent in the narrow sense that its weights are downloadable while remaining almost completely unintelligible mechanistically. Conversely, a proprietary model can expose carefully validated explanation interfaces without exposing weights. Open weights, interpretability, and explainability are therefore different dimensions.

Local explanations concern one prediction or a narrow neighborhood. Global explanations aim to characterize the model over a larger input domain. Regional explanations target a cohort, cluster, or operational regime. These scopes cannot be collapsed into one another without assumptions. A locally accurate linear surrogate may describe a decision boundary well around one instance while being useless as a global model. Conversely, a global feature-importance ranking can hide heterogeneous local behavior, interactions, and subgroup failures.

The scope matters operationally. When debugging an individual failure, local analysis is appropriate. When writing a model card, validating policy constraints, or evaluating systematic bias, global and subgroup analyses are essential. In production, a useful architecture often combines all three: global model-level diagnostics, regional monitoring for cohorts and operational modes, and local explanations for selected decisions.

The practical consequence is that XAI begins by naming the question, not by choosing a library. Once the explanatory target is explicit, the next task is to specify what evidence would actually count as an answer.
