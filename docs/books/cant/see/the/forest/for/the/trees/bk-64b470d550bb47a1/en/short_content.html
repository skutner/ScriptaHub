CHAPTER 1

The Argument in Ten Minutes

For roughly twenty-five years - essentially my entire adult life - I have kept returning to the same family of questions. Why do intelligent people build systems that later trap them? Why do institutions created to solve real problems become unable to see the problems created by their own solutions? Why does progress so often produce both freedom and dependence, clarity and blindness, resilience and fragility? I expected that one day I would write a book about this. I did not expect that trying to write it would become part of the evidence.

The immediate trigger was much smaller. While revising an earlier version of this manuscript, I asked an AI system to keep the urgency, doubt, and personal vulnerability of what I was saying. I wanted the text to admit something uncomfortable: after spending years around technology, research, startups, institutions, and AI, I am less certain than I used to be that our societies understand the systems they are building. I also wanted to admit the opposite: I still choose optimism, because the alternative is to stop looking for useful interventions before we have proved that none exist.

The AI improved the prose. It also weakened the point.

Nothing it wrote was obviously false. That was precisely the problem. It made the argument balanced, polite, and easy to accept. It removed some of the sentences that made me sound uncertain, some that made the situation sound urgent, and some that placed responsibility on people who actually make decisions. It converted a conflict into a reasonable discussion among compatible considerations. Sentence by sentence, the result was defensible. As a representation of what I meant, it was less true.

I call this smoothing. The term is provisional. I use it for a familiar social operation: remove enough sharp edges from a statement that incompatible audiences can accept it without confronting the disagreement underneath. Smoothing can be civilizing. It can reduce needless hostility, translate between vocabularies, and keep cooperation possible when complete agreement is impossible. But it can also become a subtle form of lying by omission. The hard

part disappears while the words remain correct.

AI did not invent this. It learned it from us.

For years I described parts of modern public culture as hypocritical. I now think that word is often too simple. Hypocrisy suggests that a person knows one thing and says another. Much of what I see is more sophisticated. People inhabit several roles, incentives, loyalties, and vocabularies at once. A manager may sincerely care about employees and sincerely optimize a metric that harms them. A politician may sincerely defend democratic norms and sincerely use every legal advantage that weakens those norms when the other side seems threatening. A researcher may sincerely value truth and sincerely avoid a question that threatens publication, funding, or status. The contradiction is not always hidden inside one cynical mind. It can be distributed across a system.

This is why the American saying in the title matters: can't see the forest for the trees. Modern civilization is extraordinarily good at tending trees. The founder tends a company. The investor tends a portfolio. The scientist tends a problem. The engineer tends a subsystem. The civil servant tends a mandate. The lawyer tends a category of rights or liabilities. The journalist tends a story. The citizen tends a family, a career, and a flood of immediate demands. Many of these trees are beautiful. Some are profitable. Some are morally important. Some keep people alive.

The problem is not the trees. The forest is made of trees.

The problem is that very few roles are rewarded for asking what all the trees are doing together.

That failure is not merely philosophical. It is becoming a business, political, and technological risk. Complexity has grown into one of the main resources of the modern world. We create value by mastering domains that other people cannot easily master: semiconductor fabrication, derivatives, molecular biology, cybersecurity, regulation, machine learning, logistics, tax law, standards, platform governance. Specialization is productive because knowledge is too large for one mind. But the same complexity creates rents, prestige, barriers to entry, dependence, and opportunities for obfuscation. There is a real economic literature on strategic complexity: firms can make offers harder to compare; managers can make disclosures harder to read; competition itself can create incentives to complicate choice (Spiegler, 2016; Edwards, 2019; Aghamolla and Smith, 2023).

Complexity therefore does two things at once. It allows civilization to become more capable, and it makes the consequences of capability harder to integrate.

This tension resembles, very loosely, the old yin-yang intuition that one tendency can create conditions for its apparent opposite. I do not treat yin-yang as a scientific law. Attempts to map the philosophy directly onto measurable biological pairs have not established a universal empirical theory, and recent reviews still note major gaps in mechanism and quantification (Wang, 2005; Yang et al., 2025). I use it as a warning against one-directional thinking. More local sophistication can coexist with less global comprehension. More information can coexist with less orientation. More connectivity can coexist with deeper social isolation. More control can create more fragility. More speech can make important speech harder to hear.

The last example matters because modern censorship can operate without a censor. I am not using censorship here in the legal sense. Nobody needs to ban a

book for the book to become functionally absent. Put it inside an almost infinite

competition for attention and it can disappear more effectively than if it were prohibited and therefore made interesting.

The incentive structure is not neutral. Large studies of social media have found that political out-group language predicts sharing unusually strongly, that social feedback reinforces future expressions of moral outrage, and that false stories in one major Twitter dataset spread farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than true ones (Rathje, Van Bavel, and van der Linden, 2021; Brady et al., 2021; Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral, 2018). A preregistered audit published in 2025 found that Twitter's engagement-based ranking amplified emotionally charged and out-group-hostile political content relative to a reverse-chronological feed, including content users themselves did not say they preferred (Milli et al., 2025). Evidence on echo chambers is more mixed than public rhetoric suggests, but the larger point survives the nuance: visibility is allocated by mechanisms that reward measurable response, and measurable response is not the same thing as considered value.

This creates a strange information regime. A crude statement can travel because it irritates. A false statement can travel because it is novel. A tribal statement can travel because it activates identity. A serious argument that asks the reader to hold three conflicting mechanisms in mind may lose before anyone evaluates whether it is true.

That is the environment in which this book and the other free books around it exist.

I did not begin with a plan to publish a shelf of books. I began with philosophy and literature, and with a persistent interest in limits. Over the years that interest became what I now call Outfinitism: not a finished doctrine, but a way of looking at the world that starts from a simple fact. Every useful model has a domain. Every domain has an edge. The fact that a model works brilliantly inside its domain gives it no automatic authority outside it.

The deeper substrate is meta-rationality. The map is not the territory, but that sentence is useless if it becomes an excuse for cheap relativism. Some maps are better than others for a given purpose. Some theories predict well in one regime and fail in another. Two theories can contradict each other and still be useful at different scales or under different conditions. The practical task is not to dissolve truth into perspectives. It is to develop a better discipline for knowing where a theory, metric, institution, or AI system stops deserving our confidence.

When our research programme began using AI to examine a few aspects of this problem, I thought the work would converge. We would write several books, clarify the main ideas, connect them to technical experiments and perhaps to literature, and gradually reduce the backlog of unanswered questions.

The opposite happened.

Each answer exposed another boundary. AI agents raised questions about authority. Authority raised questions about permission, memory, accountability, and sovereignty. Scientific automation raised questions about what counts as evidence, review, novelty, and explanation. Institutions led to power. Power led to attention. Attention led to trivialization, preference falsification, and the economics of outrage. Social systems led to suffering, adaptation, mental health, status, and the strange ways people remain functional inside structures they privately distrust. Literature became useful because some conflicts are easier to perceive when they are inhabited by characters than when they are compressed into a diagram.

The collection grew because the world did not respect our chapter boundaries.

I am not claiming that this makes me unusually wise. A less flattering explanation is probably more accurate. I have been lucky enough to spend a large

part of my adult life obsessed with coherence and with things that do not fit. I

notice cognitive dissonance and incompatible explanations and have difficulty leaving them alone. AI then gave that obsession leverage. It became possible to follow one inconsistency into another discipline without waiting months for the logistics of research and drafting.

Obsession is not proof.

AI also creates a new epistemic danger. A language model is very good at connecting points. Sometimes the connection is real. Sometimes coherence pressure produces the impression of an argument where the evidence only supports adjacent observations. A thousand elegant links can still inherit one hidden mistake. Evidence is correlated; quantity is not independence. The books therefore cannot be treated as a proof that Outfinitism, or any larger diagnosis, is correct. They are a research programme, a set of experiments, and a growing collection of claims that should survive criticism separately before they are allowed to support a grand conclusion.

Still, the pattern is now too persistent for me to dismiss as one hallucinated connection. We keep finding the same structural problems in different places: local optimization without integration, metrics that become targets, interfaces that hide dependency, institutions that accumulate rules faster than causal memory, markets that price what can be captured and neglect what cannot, political systems that compress millions of lives into administrable categories, and information platforms that can measure reaction more easily than reflection.

The first claim of this book is therefore modest in wording and severe in consequence: humanity's capacity to create complexity is growing faster than the unaided human capacity to understand and govern the systems created.

The second claim is biological. Human attention, working memory, time, and emotional energy are limited. Training can improve what we do with them; it cannot make them infinite. Expertise works partly by compression. The expert sees patterns that the novice must inspect one element at a time. But every compression discards something. Herbert Simon's bounded rationality remains a better starting point for real institutions than the fantasy of an actor who sees all options and consequences (Simon, 1955).

The third claim is about power. Power is partly the right to simplify reality for other people. A state defines categories. A company defines KPIs. A market turns futures into prices. A platform ranks what is visible. An investor defines what counts as a fundable company. An AI system selects context before generating an answer. The simplification is often necessary. If AI increasingly replaces local intellectual labour - drafting, coding, search, comparison, simulation, summarization, routine design - then the human scarcity moves upward. The difficult work becomes choosing the frame, seeing its edge, orchestrating processes across domains, deciding which contradiction cannot be smoothed away, and accepting responsibility for consequences that no local optimization owned.

This makes the work that motivated Outfinitism more relevant, not less. The goal is not to produce humans who know every leaf. It is to create habits, tools, institutions, and forms of education that make scale-switching normal: look closely when detail matters, look up when the local objective begins to damage the surrounding system, and state the boundary when the model no longer deserves authority.

I do not know whether this message will break through the attention barrier. That uncertainty belongs in the book. We have already discovered that making serious books free does not make them visible. Promotion is difficult precisely because the argument resists compression into one tribal slogan. It may be ignored. It may be read by a small group. It may become useful only after some of the systems discussed here fail in ways that make the vocabulary suddenly relevant.

That possibility is not an excuse for passivity. Prevention has an unfair business model: when it succeeds, the disaster that would have justified the investment never occurs. The people who remove resilience can look efficient until the shock arrives. The people who preserve it can look wasteful during every quarter in which nothing breaks.

I increasingly think this is one of the central moral problems of leadership in complex systems. We are rewarded for tending the visible tree. We are rarely rewarded for preserving the conditions under which the forest survives.

The rest of the book develops this argument from several directions: cognition, organizations, startups, markets, media, political systems, AI, investment, and the philosophy of limits. I will try not to smooth the conflicts into one happy synthesis. Some tensions are real. Democracy is both slow and resilient. Centralization is both efficient and dangerous. Complexity is both the source of modern capability and a medium through which power hides. AI is both an amplifier of confusion and the best tool we have yet built for extending the range of human inquiry.

My own position will continue to oscillate. The pessimistic case is strong. Intelligent people repeatedly fail in front of complexity, and AI is shortening the time between invention and consequence. The optimistic case is weaker as a prediction but stronger as a choice: systems can learn, people can build better institutions, distributed intelligence can outperform brittle central control, and exposing the limits of our maps can make correction possible earlier.

I did not start the experiment that is human history. None of us did. But I am inside it, benefiting from it, building technologies within it, and therefore partly responsible for what I can see.

That is the call to action in its simplest form. Tend your tree. Do it well. Then look up.
