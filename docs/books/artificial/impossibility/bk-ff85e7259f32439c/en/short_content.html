When engineering feasibility is not enough

The book began as a collection of science-fiction premises: public AI, preventive medicine, automated scientific review, non-addictive platforms, universal tutors, shared ownership of robots, and treaties restraining capabilities all participants privately fear. The examples appeared unrelated until the same structures kept returning. Useful projects could be prototyped yet lacked a payer, legitimate authority, compatible funding category, viable ownership model, or coalition capable of carrying the transition into society.

Artificial impossibility names this condition. A project is technically plausible and potentially valuable, but ordinary actors following current mandates repeatedly lack a rational path to sponsor it. Translating the project into a familiar startup, grant, procurement category, or revenue model may destroy the very mechanism that creates its value. This does not imply conspiracy or incompetence. Investors, regulators, reviewers, families, and states can each behave responsibly while their mandates combine into global absence: a feasible future with no institutional home.

A map of recurring barriers

The book offers fifteen diagnostic patterns rather than a single master theory. A Missing Sponsor appears when benefits are real but cross budgets, jurisdictions, or generations. A Power-Law Orphan may become useful and sustainable without offering venture-scale returns. A Success Penalty afflicts cures, prevention, durable products, and services intended to make themselves unnecessary. A Commons Penalty arises when openness, neutrality, or interoperability creates social value precisely by preventing one owner from capturing it.

Other patterns concern proof, authority, and opposition. The Proof Trap requires evidence that can be produced only after denied permission. The Legibility Tax punishes projects crossing administrative categories. The Responsibility Vacuum appears when a capability arrives before any actor has legitimate authority to accept its consequences. Concentrated losers exercise Veto Asymmetry against diffuse beneficiaries; rivals enter Restraint Races; abundance meets a Positional Ceiling in status goods; Sacred Boundaries protect personhood or autonomy; and the Smoothing Veil hides contradictions that an institution needs not to see.

Selection systems have characteristic shadows

The same technology can be feasible under one ownership structure and impossible under another. An enterprise model sold as a service may attract conventional capital, while a neutral and auditable public model needs infrastructure finance and governance. An AI reviewer sold as productivity software has customers; one empowered to allocate public grants requires appeal, legitimacy, and accountable human authority. The correct unit of analysis is therefore not the technology alone but the project inside a selection environment.

Markets, venture funds, grant systems, procurement rules, regulators, professions, families, and governments each solve genuine problems. Each also notices only what its categories, time horizons, and duties make legible. Artificial impossibility appears in their shadows, especially when several systems reject a project for different locally rational reasons. The map helps replace vague accusations—investors lack vision, reviewers resist innovation, the public fears progress—with sharper questions about who benefits, who pays, which right constrains action, who absorbs error, and what must remain open.

Capital, public funding, and value that erases itself

Venture capital is structurally designed for portfolios in which a small number of extreme successes dominate returns. That makes it powerful for scalable, ownable opportunities and poorly fitted to low-margin infrastructure, slow research, accessibility tools, or projects whose benefits disperse. Calling these projects bad startups may be accurate without making them bad projects. Different capital—procurement, patient finance, utilities, cooperatives, endowments, public options, or purpose trusts—may be needed because the desired outcome does not fit a venture fund’s mathematics.

Public systems face a different constraint: they must make choices legible and defensible. Categories, reviewers, eligibility rules, and comparable proposals protect fairness, yet they can reject work that changes the question or cannot provide evidence before experimentation. AI may lower the cost of review without resolving normative authority. The book therefore favors portfolios of microgrants, staged evidence funding, qualified lotteries, investigator-centered awards, replication, and mission programs. Funding becomes a series of experiments under public priorities, not a machine claiming to predict scientific success with impossible precision.

Vetoes, races, and the limits of abundance

Even when a project creates aggregate benefit, organized incumbents may bear immediate losses of income, authority, competence, or identity. Diffuse future beneficiaries rarely mobilize with equal intensity. Treating resistance as ignorance misses legitimate transition costs and encourages brittle reform. Compensation, retraining, phased standards, buyouts, shared ownership, and conversion of existing institutions can protect people and socially valuable capabilities without granting every incumbent a permanent right to block change.

Some barriers cannot be solved within one jurisdiction. In a restraint race, every actor may prefer mutual limitation while fearing that unilateral restraint transfers advantage to competitors. Verification and shared liability become as important as moral intention. Abundance also fails to multiply ranking, prestige, exclusive location, admiration, or romantic desirability. Material productivity may therefore intensify positional conflict rather than dissolve it. The book treats work, merit, family, inherited advantage, and sacred ideas of personhood as real institutional forces, not irrational debris awaiting technical correction.

Smoothing, movable limits, and fitted remedies

Smoothing is the book’s working term for the way institutions preserve coherent stories by omitting losers, external costs, temporal effects, contradictory goals, or alternatives that do not fit the accepted frame. AI can industrialize this tendency, generating polished proposals and summaries that make contested choices look technically inevitable. Anti-smoothing systems would identify unrepresented actors, distinguish evidence from value judgments, expose conditions under which a recommendation reverses, preserve rejected alternatives, and trigger special scrutiny when a proposal promises benefits without identifiable costs.

There is no universal cure. Availability payments can reward capabilities society hopes to use rarely, as with conserved antibiotics. Advance commitments and milestone prizes can pay before an ordinary market exists. Digital public infrastructure can finance a neutral foundation while firms compete above it. Purpose trusts, utilities, mutuals, cooperatives, and social wealth funds distribute control differently. Each remedy introduces new risks of capture, stagnation, gaming, or exclusion. An Outfinitist institution must therefore monitor both the barrier it was created to move and the new barrier it may produce.

Why read the complete book

A ten-minute synthesis compresses a detailed pattern language and necessarily omits the evidence used to bound its claims. The complete book examines venture decision-making, grant review, public goods, prevention, antibiotics, compute concentration, strategic competition, status research, institutional legitimacy, and AI sycophancy. It labels when an argument rests on empirical findings, institutional inference, or a speculative scenario. Those distinctions are essential because the map is offered as a research vocabulary, not a set of laws or predictions.

The full work also shows how patterns combine within concrete project designs. Its cabinet ranges from replication facilities and public scientific compute to care robotics, consumption fiduciaries, robot dividends, future-generation chambers, rights for potentially sentient systems, and verification for international restraint. For each, the missing details concern ownership, evidence, transition, appeal, metrics, and termination. Reading the complete book turns the memorable labels into a practical discipline for asking whether a boundary protects a right, reflects nature, or merely preserves an unexamined institutional arrangement.
