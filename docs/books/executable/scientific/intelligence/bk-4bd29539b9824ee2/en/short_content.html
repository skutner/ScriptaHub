From scientific prose to semantic execution

The book begins from a productive dissatisfaction with the scientific document. Articles remain indispensable because prose can express novelty, qualification, unresolved concepts, and interpretive judgment before a field possesses stable formal vocabulary. Yet prose is computationally insufficient: claims, assumptions, datasets, code, exclusions, and methods are dispersed across artefacts, while rerunning a script does not necessarily reconstruct the argument that gave its output significance. Existing notebooks, workflows, research-object packages, provenance standards, knowledge graphs, and nanopublications solve important portions of reproducibility and machine actionability. The proposed missing layer is semantic execution: an inspectable structure connecting questions and claims to evidence, assumptions, transformations, checks, and stopping conditions. This is not a plan to replace articles with programs. It would give formalizable portions executable companions while explicitly marking everything that still depends on interpretation. The intended gain is not merely repeated numbers, but the ability to ask which conclusions change when a source, baseline, method, or assumption changes.

Why fluent scientific agents need divided authority

Contemporary agents can search literature, propose hypotheses, write and run code, analyse results, and draft manuscripts. Their danger lies in concentrated epistemic authority: the same model may interpret the task, select evidence, judge its own performance, and narrate why the outcome is credible. Tool logs do not necessarily expose the semantic status of intermediate values, and a convincing report can conceal missing alternatives or a search stopped by budget rather than adequacy. The book proposes a governed composition instead of a single artificial scientist. Neural models remain useful for difficult interpretation, synthesis, and candidate generation. Deterministic components, formal engines, reviewers, and institutions receive distinct responsibilities. Source discovery is not support; a citation is not proof; rerunning is not replication; parser acceptance is not semantic fidelity. The architecture matters because these distinctions become enforceable rather than advisory. Automation can contribute rapidly without automatically acquiring the authority to promote its own proposals into knowledge.

SOP Lang as an intermediate representation

SOP Lang is positioned neither as unrestricted executable English nor as one universal logic. It is a compact, human-inspectable intermediate representation beneath possible natural-language front ends and above heterogeneous back ends such as databases, solvers, proof assistants, statistical tools, neural models, and ordinary code. Its kernel recognizes declarations, produced values, literal arguments, and two kinds of reference. Ordinary references carry semantic values; meta-references can expose producer identity, provenance, alternatives, cache state, or validation records under policy. Individual commands own their argument grammars, allowing a statistical, regulatory, or literary command to speak its domain language without bloating the universal parser. This minimality creates a real trade-off: extensibility improves, but static analysis depends on rigorous command contracts. Commands must declare accepted inputs, results, effects, determinism, cost, and failure behavior. The scientific contribution therefore resides less in surface syntax than in whether this small kernel can support precise composition, inspection, and evolution across implementations.

Dynamic circuits and bounded construction

A request does not execute an entire persistent knowledge model. It should activate a small dependency circuit containing only the values, commands, templates, and knowledge shards relevant to the current goal. Explicit def-use relationships support scheduling, caching, incremental recomputation, alternatives, subcircuits, and provenance. Dynamic construction permits the circuit to change as values appear, methods fail, counterexamples emerge, or ambiguity remains unresolved. Crucially, the constructor is proposed as a bounded planner over indexed capabilities, not a free-form program generator. Compiled descriptors identify which templates can produce required capabilities; deterministic applicability checks enforce types and policy; new values activate only local additions; explicit budgets constrain search. This is the programme’s central unresolved scientific problem. It is not yet known whether semantic applicability can be summarized cheaply enough to select appropriate circuits from language-model-scale libraries. If the constructor must inspect a large fraction of stored templates for every request, the architecture’s claimed scalability fails.

Persistent competence without combinatorial explosion

The symbolic model’s prospective advantage is sparse activation: most stored facts and procedures should remain inactive for any one request. The book separates several explosion risks rather than hiding them behind a single search parameter. Large knowledge stores require compiled indexes, immutable shards, lazy loading, compact handles, and eviction. Large template libraries require small capability descriptors, goal-directed retrieval, subsumption, and conservative dominance pruning. Recursive work requires memoization and explicit fixpoint detection. Competing interpretations must remain distinct alternatives unless evidence or policy justifies selection; formal equivalences alone may use canonicalization or e-graph techniques. Budgets are semantic inputs, since a short run and a long run may legitimately produce different guarantees. A constructive witness can establish a positive result early, while a negative or exhaustive result may require complete exploration. If time expires, the proper status is incomplete or unknown—not false. Activation efficiency, completion status, and the fraction of the library examined therefore matter more than raw answer rate.

Provenance, abstention, and enforceable trust boundaries

Every produced value can carry its source, producer, command version, dependencies, and execution status, but provenance is not truth. A faithful trace can lead from a false source to a false conclusion. The architecture accordingly preserves non-equivalent epistemic statuses: quoted, inferred, proposed, statistically estimated, solver-verified, contradicted, promoted, or unresolved. Abstention is a positive capability when language falls outside a profile, knowledge is missing, alternatives remain open, a search frontier is truncated, or validation fails. Governance further separates proposal from promotion. A coding agent may author a candidate command or circuit, but compilation, sandboxing, tests, independent review, and institutional approval determine whether that artefact can enter an authoritative registry. These mechanisms can strengthen transparency, reproducibility, security policy, and accountability; they do not automatically establish scientific validity, fairness, privacy, legal compliance, or social legitimacy. The book consistently presents trustworthiness as a property of scoped mechanisms and institutions, not of readable syntax itself.

What the prototypes establish—and do not

Three experimental lines ground the proposal. SOPLangCircuits demonstrates persistent catalogues, selective loading, bounded branch execution, and the distinction between positive witnesses and completeness-sensitive answers. dynamicCircuits treats coding agents as slow-path compilers of documents and task requirements into candidate SOP artefacts, while a separate runtime owns official execution and receipts. eSLM investigates controlled language, declarative knowledge, bounded symbolic methods, explicit witnesses, abstention, strategy contracts, and strict authority gates. Together they demonstrate implementable pieces, not a finished architecture. They differ over syntax profiles, storage, security, language coverage, and how much knowledge should execute. The proposed next step is unification through shared contracts and benchmarks rather than mechanically merging codebases. Still open are a stable cross-implementation kernel, broad natural-language handling, transfer of learned circuits, multi-method verification, ontology evolution, and efficient construction over millions or billions of artefacts. The programme’s hypotheses remain credible precisely because these falsification conditions are made explicit.

Why read the complete book

The complete work provides the technical and evidential qualifications needed to judge whether dynamic SOP circuits are more than an attractive metaphor. It compares graph, workflow, compiler, logic, cognitive, probabilistic, cryptographic, provenance, and research-object traditions without pretending that any one already solves the whole problem. Detailed treatment of values, references, command-owned grammars, alternatives, templates, budgets, indexes, receipts, authority roles, and prototype differences reveals where guarantees genuinely arise and where uncertainty merely moves to another interface. The use cases—peer review, plural literary interpretation, specification generation, and agentic systems—also test different notions of evidence rather than illustrating one narrow workflow repeatedly. Appendices supply the reference model and conceptual prerequisites, while the experimental programme defines baselines, measurements, maturity levels, and disconfirming outcomes. Those particulars are essential for deciding where this research instrument may be useful, uneconomical, or fundamentally limited.
