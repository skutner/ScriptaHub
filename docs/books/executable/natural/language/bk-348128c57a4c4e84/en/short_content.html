The formalisation gap

Large language models make natural language a practical interface to computation, but fluent prose remains an unstable execution surface. A model can silently change quantifier scope, merge identities, resolve an ambiguity, invent a bridge premise, or promote reported evidence into accepted fact before calling an exact solver. The solver may then calculate correctly over the wrong representation. Execution relocates some errors from arithmetic and search into formalisation; it does not eliminate them. The danger increases when a precise numerical or logical result conceals the uncertainty of the translation that produced its premises. Exact downstream computation can make an upstream semantic mistake look more authoritative, not less.

The book therefore demands a visible trust boundary among source statement, candidate interpretation, evidence, semantic profile, executable artifact, result, and rendered explanation. Proof establishes a consequence of a formal expression, not fidelity to the original language. Fidelity requires source anchors, alternatives, tests, review, and a translation record. The defensible guarantee is deliberately bounded: given named revisions, profiles, assumptions, implementations, and execution limits, a canonical result follows reproducibly. That is weaker than full machine understanding but stronger than confidence in eloquent output. It also makes disagreement localizable. Reviewers can ask whether the source was mistranslated, an operator was poorly specified, or a backend violated its contract instead of treating the whole pipeline as one opaque act.

English and the price of thought

English is chosen as a practical source language because of its role in science, software, regulation, and model training, not because it is a universal grammar. Its analytic structure efficiently uses word order, auxiliaries, pronouns, articles, prepositions, and lexical context. That economy permits pervasive underspecification. Identity, reference, tense, aspect, modality, attachment, possession, collective action, and even uses of “be” or “same” can conceal distinctions that matter when a sentence becomes a durable knowledge object or instruction. Human readers often repair these gaps from context without noticing the work involved. Execution forces those quiet interpretive choices to become consequential, persistent, and potentially reproducible across many later operations.

Cross-linguistic comparison supplies design evidence rather than a hierarchy of languages. Evidentiality, inclusive and exclusive “we,” participant continuity, temporal remoteness, possession types, perspective, spatial frames, and social deixis show that distinctions optional in English can be learnable and ordinary elsewhere. The book rejects strong linguistic determinism and warns about cultural confounds. Its more measured principle is that language helps determine the price of thought: possible distinctions become easier or costlier to express, repeat, institutionalize, and compile. SLEnglish accordingly borrows design questions, not surface grammar wholesale. A distinction deserves inclusion when making it explicit improves auditability or execution, not merely because another language grammatically marks it.

A missing grammar for systems

Philosophical and systems problems add another layer. A cloud, organization, process, legal entity, or dataset may persist while its parts change, so identity must be qualified by criteria, context, scale, and time. Groups require especially careful treatment. An aggregate, crowd, network, swarm, organization, institution, and legal person support different forms of coordination and agency. Saying that “the group decided” may refer to authorized procedure, emergent behavior, statistical regularity, or convenient fiction. Treating those cases as interchangeable can allocate authority or responsibility to the wrong entity. An executable representation must record what continuity criterion and agency model license the group-level claim.

Causation and responsibility likewise cannot be compressed safely into one grammatical subject. A harm may involve direct action, enabling infrastructure, institutional incentives, delayed effects, distributed maintenance, omitted safeguards, and unequal benefits or burdens. Making these layers explicit does not let language decide guilt. It makes omissions contestable and supports more precise interventions. Candidate operators for boundaries, reversibility, emergence, causal depth, responsibility, unknown unknowns, option value, and systemic effects are conceptual engineering proposals whose usefulness must be empirically tested. Their value lies in forcing questions that ordinary prose can hide. Their danger is false precision: an elaborate operator name may suggest that a disputed concept has already acquired a settled formal meaning.

What SLEnglish proposes

SLEnglish, or SOP Lang English, uses one regular outer statement shape: a stable single-assignment identity, a path-addressed operator, named parameters, typed values, contexts, and explicit references. Semantic diversity lives in versioned operator contracts rather than an ever-growing parser. Files declare required roles, types, cardinality, determinism, effects, compatible profiles, canonical ordering, tests, rendering guidance, and migration history. A directory of suggestive names without such contracts would be only a vocabulary, not an executable language. The regular shell is intended to lower parsing ambiguity while allowing communities to extend meaning through inspectable libraries. Extensibility therefore moves into governance: operators must be reviewed, versioned, compared, and retired without silently changing older programs.

The architecture keeps proposition identity separate from commitment to truth and distinguishes references to a node from references to its produced value. Contexts can represent actual, reported, hypothetical, legal, or simulated worlds. Rules and queries state coverage and closure assumptions, allowing “unknown” to remain different from “false” or “not supplied.” Competing communities may publish different ontologies and bridge them partially rather than agree on one taxonomy. Correction locality is a principal hoped-for benefit: reviewers could revise one identity, evidence mode, or scope relation while preserving unaffected dependencies. This is especially useful when a statement must be quoted without endorsement or tested inside a scenario. The system can preserve who asserted what, under which context, without collapsing report, belief, and accepted fact.

Compilation, execution, and executable science

An LLM may propose a compilation from unrestricted prose, but its output remains a candidate. It must include source spans, unresolved references, alternatives, introduced assumptions, and unsupported constructions. Deterministic parsing, path resolution, schema validation, type checking, context checks, provenance checks, round-trip rendering, paraphrase comparison, and semantic mutation tests then expose different failure classes. Translation confidence is not truth probability, and automatic repair must not silently alter malformed meaning. Human review remains necessary where ambiguity affects substantive conclusions. The architecture’s contribution is to give that review inspectable objects and targeted tests rather than asking a reviewer to trust or reject an entire generated formalization at once.

No single backend defines the language. Compatible fragments may lower to rules, graphs, RDF systems, databases, temporal engines, constraint solvers, theorem provers, simulations, generated code, or dynamic semantic circuits. Each adapter declares preserved semantics, approximations, and rejected constructs. Applied to science, this becomes a semantic build system linking concepts, protocols, data, code, analyses, claims, reviews, and corrections. When an input changes or a source is retracted, affected dependencies can be identified without pretending that every downstream claim is automatically false. The build-system analogy emphasizes incremental work: changed evidence should trigger focused revalidation. It also prevents backend success from laundering unsupported constructs that the target formalism cannot faithfully express.

Why read the complete book

The complete work contains the specification-level substance necessarily compressed here: canonical grammar, parameter registry, candidate operator library, cross-linguistic mappings, worked programs, backend contracts, evaluation matrix, and critical reading map. Those materials show how evidence chains, clusivity, process identity, measurement, ambiguity, collective agency, systemic risk, and what-if queries would share one syntax. They also expose the implementation burden and places where an elegant concept may fragment into unusable microdialects. The worked examples are essential because the proposal succeeds only if its regularity survives difficult language without hiding complexity in undocumented operators. They let readers inspect whether correction is genuinely local and whether rendered output remains intelligible to domain reviewers.

Equally important, the book positions SLEnglish against mature alternatives rather than declaring them obsolete. RDF, OWL, SHACL, SPARQL, PROV, Common Logic, controlled natural languages, Datalog, answer-set programming, solvers, workflows, and research objects serve as baselines and interoperable targets. The research programme specifies rejection conditions and measures false assurance, translation fidelity, correction cost, review effort, source traceability, and backend behavior. Reading the full proposal is necessary to judge whether its added semantic layer genuinely improves auditability or merely recreates capabilities better supplied by established formalisms. That comparative and falsifiable posture is the book-specific reason to continue: SLEnglish is offered as a research hypothesis whose value must be demonstrated against existing tools, not inferred from the attractiveness of executable language alone.
