Chapter 1 - The Creature Above the Individual

Imagine a hospital, an army, a bank, and a social platform placed side by side. Their purposes differ so radically that any moral comparison can become silly. Yet each has the same basic problem: thousands of local decisions must become one coherent action. The nurse sees one patient; the procurement office sees a contract; the board sees a budget; the regulator sees a category. No single participant sees the institution exactly as the outside world experiences it. The institution exists in the conversion between these viewpoints.

That conversion is where this book begins. Before asking whether history is egregopathic, we need to know what an egregopath would be. The word must do more than insult organizations we dislike. It needs a functional definition, a boundary around the concept, and a way to distinguish ordinary institutional failure from a durable architecture that repeatedly converts people into instruments while preserving a convincing moral self-description. This chapter builds that vocabulary slowly, because without it every later historical example would be rhetoric masquerading as explanation.

A Name for the Thing

The word is deliberately artificial. "Egregopathy" borrows the suggestive shape of egregore, a modern term for a group-form imagined as if it had a life beyond its members, and joins it to -pathy, not to claim a supernatural group mind but to name a recurrent dysfunction of collective agency. The point is operational. We need a word for a pattern that otherwise dissolves into dozens of disciplines: organizational misconduct, state crime, moral disengagement, collective narcissism, bureaucratic violence, institutional betrayal, extractive institutions, dehumanization, unethical pro-organizational behavior, group agency, normalization of deviance, and the ordinary administration of extraordinary harm.

Without a common word, the patterns remain academically separated. A psychologist studies the individual who obeys. A sociologist studies the organization. A historian studies the regime. A political scientist studies extraction. A business scholar studies misconduct. A philosopher asks whether the group can be responsible. A computer scientist asks whether a multi-agent system is aligned. Each sees one surface of the same possible object.

RESEARCH The analogy with psychopathy is not chosen because psychopathy means "evil." It does not. Modern psychopathy research concerns a cluster of traits and behavioral tendencies that can include callousness, manipulativeness, shallow affect, irresponsibility, impulsivity, and antagonism. The literature increasingly treats these properties dimensionally rather than as evidence of a separate human taxon. Research on the broader "Dark Factor" of personality similarly suggests a common dispositional core behind several socially aversive traits: the tendency to maximize one's utility while disregarding, accepting, or provoking disutility for others, supported by beliefs that justify the behavior (Moshagen, Hilbig, & Zettler, 2018).

For groups, the relevant analogy is narrower. An organization cannot literally have shallow affect if it has no affect. It can, however, produce behavior in which the suffering of others has little or no negative weight unless that suffering threatens the organization's goals. It cannot feel guilt, but it can possess error-correction mechanisms. If those mechanisms activate strongly for financial loss and weakly for human harm, the behavioral asymmetry is real whether or not the organization feels anything.

The proposed construct therefore begins with observable features rather than inner states.

An egregopathic collective shows persistent instrumentalization: persons become labor units, risk categories, market segments, voters, enemies, collateral damage, liabilities, users, bodies, cases, or data points. It shows boundary morality: protection and moral concern are distributed unevenly according to membership, usefulness, status, or strategic relevance. It shows responsibility dispersion: the architecture makes it difficult for any single node to experience full ownership of the output. It shows conscience simulation: ethical language, apology, ritual, compliance, philanthropy, or public values can be generated without corresponding changes in core incentives. It shows harm externalization: the collective captures the benefit while people outside the decision boundary absorb a disproportionate share of the cost. Finally, it shows self-preserving moral adaptation: scandal produces reputation repair, procedural revision, personnel replacement, or narrative reframing more readily than abandonment of the objective that generated the harm.

None of these properties proves malevolent intention. That is precisely the point.

INFERENCE The concept becomes useful when it distinguishes three cases that ordinary moral language tends to collapse.

In the first, a pathological leader captures an otherwise ordinary institution. Here the individual diagnosis matters most. In the second, a coalition of consciously malicious actors coordinates harm. This is conspiracy in the literal sense. In the third, and most interesting, the harmful behavioral personality exists at the level of the system even though no stable set of participants individually possesses it.

Imagine a corporation in which every executive personally prefers not to injure customers, yet each also faces a narrow metric. Product teams are rewarded for release speed. Sales is rewarded for volume. Legal is rewarded for defensibility. Finance is rewarded for margin. Public relations is rewarded for reputation. The board is rewarded for return. If harms arrive slowly, probabilistically, and outside the accounting period, no individual preference against harm needs to be reversed. It merely needs to lose every local contest against a metric with institutional teeth.

The resulting organization may behave more ruthlessly than any of its members.

This is not metaphorical in the weak sense. The company can have a stable preference ordering instantiated in budgets, promotion criteria, policies, software, contracts, and reporting hierarchies. Replace the employees and the pattern can remain. Indeed, employees who resist it may leave, be excluded from promotion, or learn to translate objections into the language the system rewards.

The organism persists by selecting its cells.

That is the first reason the concept of egregopathy deserves to exist. Individual moral psychology cannot explain a behavioral pattern whose continuity survives individual replacement.

The second reason is responsibility. If every harm is translated back into isolated personal fault, organizations gain a peculiar immunity. The whole is always innocent because only persons can be guilty, while each person is partially innocent because only the whole produced the result. List and Pettit's account of group agency identifies exactly why this can create a responsibility deficit: sometimes individuals are excused by the structure of their roles even when the group itself satisfies meaningful conditions for agency and control (List & Pettit, 2011).

RESEARCH The empirical neighborhood around the proposed concept has become denser. In 2025, Fida and colleagues validated "organizational moral disengagement" as a multilevel construct across four studies, including more than three thousand workers nested in 113 organizations. Their result matters because it shifts the conversation away from the idea that unethical behavior is simply the sum of individually unethical employees. When members perceive that the organization collectively suspends moral constraints - through justification, normalization, silence, and exoneration - unethical pro-organizational behavior becomes more likely (Fida et al., 2025). An updated review of moral decision-making in organizations likewise emphasizes that moral outcomes depend on interpersonal and organizational conditions as well as individual traits (Kouchaki & Smith, 2025).

Egregopathy is broader than organizational moral disengagement, but it should not compete with it. Moral disengagement describes a mechanism by which ordinary moral self-regulation is disabled or bypassed. Egregopathy names a system-level pattern that may recruit that mechanism together with others: asymmetric representation of stakeholders, externalization of harm, strategic control of information, persistence of objectives across personnel changes, and a weak capacity for costly self-correction. The distinction matters. A firm can have a morally disengaged team without becoming durably egregopathic, just as an egregopathic institution can sometimes act through participants who personally retain strong moral standards.

INFERENCE This also gives the concept a failure condition. If every instance called egregopathy can be explained more precisely by an existing construct without loss, the new word is unnecessary. If, however, the concept helps predict where harms will recur despite personnel turnover, apologies, compliance programs, and public scandal, it may be useful as a systems concept. Its value would then lie not in claiming that a group literally has a disorder, but in identifying architectures in which moral feedback is persistently weaker than strategic feedback.

Figure 1. The Egregopathic Loop A systems hypothesis, not a clinical diagnosis.

Figure 1 summarizes the causal story the rest of the book will test. A collective acquires a goal; the world is simplified into metrics and categories; labor is divided; distance from consequences grows; harms are exported or discounted; legitimacy protects the objective; and the resulting gains reinforce the original goal. The loop is not inevitable. Law, dissent, transparency, exit, competition, professional ethics, democratic oversight, and direct contact with affected people can interrupt it. The concept becomes interesting only where those brakes are weaker than the reinforcing loop.

The diagram should be read as a sequence of vulnerabilities rather than as a mathematical model. No quantity is being smuggled in. There is no hidden "egregopathy score" that converts history into a decimal. The point is simpler: if the same sequence appears in very different domains, it gives us a common language for asking where to intervene.

Egregopathy names the shape of that deficit when it becomes a durable strategy.

When Nobody Is the Psychopath

The comforting theory of atrocity is that terrible outcomes require terrible people. It preserves a bright border between normal life and evil. The archive is less cooperative.

Christopher Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 remains disturbing because many of the men who participated in mass shootings and deportations in occupied Poland were not selected as elite ideological killers. Browning describes ordinary middle-aged policemen operating under authority, peer pressure, role adaptation, and changing moral norms. Some eagerly killed, some complied, and a minority avoided direct participation. The system did not require universal sadism. It required enough participation, enough coordination, and enough normalization (Browning, 1992).

Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments became famous for a related reason, although later scholarship has complicated the popular story that people simply become passive instruments of authority. Reicher, Haslam, and Smith argue that obedience in the Milgram paradigm is better understood partly as "engaged followership": participants cooperate when they identify with the scientific project and its authority. That revision makes the institutional problem worse, not better. People may do harmful things not because their agency disappears, but because their agency is recruited into a valued collective purpose (Milgram, 1963; Reicher, Haslam, & Smith, 2012).

RESEARCH Moral disengagement theory provides mechanisms by which ordinary self-regulation is suspended without eliminating moral self-conception. Harmful conduct can be morally justified, described euphemistically, compared advantageously with worse conduct, displaced onto authority, diffused across a group, minimized in consequence, blamed on victims, or facilitated by dehumanization. At work, moral disengagement is associated with unethical conduct, and contextual variables such as abusive supervision and organizational politics can increase it (Bandura, 1999; Detert et al., 2008; Newman et al., 2020).

The architecture matters because each mechanism can be implemented socially.

A euphemism can become an official term. Displacement of responsibility can become a job description. Diffusion of responsibility can become a committee. Minimization can become a reporting threshold. Dehumanization can become a classification system. Moral justification can become a mission statement. Advantageous comparison can become a press release explaining that competitors are worse.

The organization does not need to persuade every participant to abandon morality. It needs to make the morally relevant description of the action difficult to encounter during the action itself.

Ann Tenbrunsel and David Messick call one part of this process "ethical fading": the moral dimensions of a decision recede while it is coded as a business, legal, technical, or strategic problem. The language is crucial. A person is not denied treatment; a case fails eligibility. A family is not expelled; a removal order is executed. A worker is not exposed to a known hazard; risk remains within tolerance. Civilians are not killed; a target package produced collateral effects. A customer is not manipulated; conversion is optimized. No phrase is necessarily dishonest. That is why the mechanism works (Tenbrunsel & Messick, 2004).

We can now state a stronger proposition: the optimal egregopathic institution may actively prefer non-psychopathic members.

A workforce composed entirely of impulsive, deceitful, antagonistic individuals would be difficult to coordinate. Recent meta-analytic evidence on psychopathy in the workplace suggests substantial costs: lower task performance and organizational citizenship behavior, and greater counterproductive behavior, particularly for secondary psychopathy (Roth & Klehe, 2025). Earlier research on psychopathy and leadership likewise finds no simple story in which psychopaths are superior leaders; the relationship with leadership emergence is small and effectiveness may suffer (Landay, Harms, & Credé, 2019).

An institution seeking durable extraction has reasons to prefer reliable accountants, diligent engineers, loyal soldiers, conscientious administrators, and sincere public servants. It needs trust internally even when it exploits externally. It needs people who keep promises to the organization while breaking promises the organization has made to outsiders. It benefits from genuine empathy inside the protected boundary because empathy improves cooperation. What it must control is where that empathy is allowed to matter.

This reverses the popular image. The dangerous system is not necessarily a nest of psychopaths. It may be an ecosystem of decent people whose decency has been partitioned.

The individual psychopath has a coordination problem: others do not trust him. The egregopathic collective solves that problem by building an interior zone of trust and an exterior zone of instrumentality. It can be warm inwardly and cold outwardly. It can reward loyalty, sacrifice, friendship, courage, and care among members while producing severe harm beyond its moral perimeter.

RESEARCH The strongest recent evidence against the simplistic "psychopaths rise to the top" story is useful precisely because it strengthens the institutional thesis. Roth and Klehe's 2025 meta-analysis synthesized 166 samples with nearly fifty thousand participants. Psychopathy was associated, on average, with lower task performance and lower organizational citizenship behavior and with more counterproductive work behavior. The effect was not a secret superpower waiting to be rewarded by capitalism; it was often organizationally costly (Roth & Klehe, 2025). Earlier meta-analytic work on leadership similarly found only a small association with leadership emergence and a negative relationship with leadership effectiveness (Landay, Harms, & Crede, 2019).

That matters because a truly efficient egregopathic organization may prefer something subtler than a workforce of psychopaths. It may need cooperation, discipline, empathy within the team, attention to reputation, and reliable execution. What it selectively rewards is not necessarily clinical callousness. It may reward compartmentalization: the ability to be warm inwardly and instrumental outwardly, to care about colleagues while treating remote stakeholders as variables, and to translate morally difficult outcomes into the professional vocabulary of optimization, compliance, security, or necessity.

This is one reason historical perpetrators are so often disappointing as monsters. Christopher Browning's study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 remains important not because it proves that ideology is irrelevant, but because it shows a killing organization populated substantially by men who were not selected as elite sadists. Conformity, role adaptation, wartime brutalization, antisemitism, authority, and peer dynamics combined inside an institutional mission. Later scholarship has refined Browning's picture and emphasized variation in indoctrination and ideology across units, but the central organizational lesson survives: mass harm can be staffed by ordinary social motives (Browning, 1992; Browning, 2025).

INFERENCE The system-level danger is therefore a composition problem. A trait that is maladaptive in an individual can still resemble the output of a successful institution. An individual liar is distrusted; an organization can distribute misleading claims across legal review, advertising, selective disclosure, and technically accurate fragments until no single statement looks like a lie. An impulsive individual is risky; an institution can be patient for decades. A remorseless individual may alienate allies; an institution can hire empathic people to manage relationships while preserving an objective that no single relationship is allowed to overturn.

The egregopath is not the average of its members. It is the function that appears after their roles are composed.

That is why moral character tests administered to individuals can miss the object. The organization can pass through them cell by cell.

The Moral Turing Test

Imagine that you cannot inspect the inner life of a powerful entity. You can only observe what it says, what it measures, what it rewards, what it hides, how it reacts when caught, and what happens to people who obstruct its goals.

This is already our situation with organizations. A corporation has no interview in which it reveals its true feelings. A state does not have a private diary. A military has doctrine and archives, not a unified conscience. The most sensible test is behavioral.

The "moral Turing test" proposed here is deliberately asymmetric. It does not ask whether an organization can produce ethical language. That test is trivial. Organizations have produced moral language for millennia. It asks whether moral information can reliably alter the organization's behavior when doing so imposes a real cost on a central objective.

RESEARCH This distinction is supported by research on organizational identification and unethical pro-organizational behavior. Employees can engage in unethical acts precisely because they identify strongly with the organization and believe the behavior helps it. Loyalty is therefore not the opposite of wrongdoing. Under some conditions, loyalty is a transmission mechanism for wrongdoing (Umphress et al., 2010).

Collective narcissism provides a parallel at the group-identity level. Golec de Zavala and colleagues define it as an unrealistic belief in the greatness of the in-group accompanied by resentment that others fail to recognize that greatness. Across studies, collective narcissism predicts intergroup hostility, sensitivity to criticism, conspiracy beliefs, and retaliatory responses. Importantly, this is distinct from secure satisfaction with one's group. A group can be loved without demanding that outsiders validate its superiority; the pathological pattern appears when image defense becomes morally privileged (Golec de Zavala et al., 2009, 2013; Marchlewska et al., 2020).

The moral Turing test therefore looks for costly constraint rather than declarations.

Does evidence of harm change targets, budgets, incentives, or decision rights? Does the institution preserve whistleblowers who threaten its reputation? Does it record harms that create legal exposure when no regulator forces it to? Does it allow people outside the organization to veto actions that impose irreversible costs on them? Does the organization maintain the same moral standard when the victim is outside its identity boundary? Does an apology reduce the probability of recurrence, or merely the probability of reputational damage?

INFERENCE A group that repeatedly fails these tests may behave as if it has cognitive empathy without affective empathy. It can understand pain exquisitely and use that understanding strategically. Marketing departments model desire. Political campaigns model fear. Intelligence agencies model adversaries. Platforms model attention. Negotiators model weakness. None of these activities is immoral by itself. Cognitive perspective-taking is one of civilization's most productive tools.

But when understanding another mind improves exploitation without imposing a corresponding cost for the other's suffering, the functional resemblance to psychopathic manipulation becomes uncomfortable.

This is why public ethics can coexist with private optimization. The institution does not need to be hypocritical in the human sense. Different subsystems can be sincere. The sustainability team can sincerely want lower emissions. The legal team can sincerely want compliance. The sales team can sincerely want growth. The board can sincerely want shareholder value. The organization as a whole can still settle into a policy that each subsystem would reject if forced to own the entire moral result.

The moral Turing test is therefore not a purity test. It is a test of integration.

A healthy collective must possess a mechanism that makes another person's suffering causally legible at the same level where strategy is chosen. If the harm appears only in a report downstream, while reward is assigned upstream, the system has built an artificial lesion between consequence and control.

Egregopathy begins there: not where nobody cares, but where caring is structurally unable to govern.

The distinction has practical consequences. Institutions frequently answer moral criticism by increasing the amount of caring language in circulation. They publish principles, fund training, add advisory committees, and create channels through which concern can be expressed. These measures can be valuable, but they do not by themselves reconnect concern to control. A system can become more articulate about suffering while remaining equally unable to let suffering change a protected objective.

A stricter test asks where veto power sits. If the people who understand the harm can only recommend, while the people who control the objective are rewarded for preserving it, the lesion remains. Moral knowledge without decision rights is institutional empathy without agency. This is why some of the most ethical people inside harmful systems become exhausted rather than effective: the organization uses them as sensors but not as governors.

RESEARCH Philosophers disagree about whether corporations and other collectives are literally moral agents. The disagreement is not superficial. Some accounts argue that sufficiently organized groups can form intentions, act for reasons, preserve commitments, and therefore bear forms of responsibility distinct from the responsibility of their members. Others argue that moral responsibility ultimately requires capacities that only human persons possess and that organizations should instead be held legally and politically accountable (Sepinwall, 2016; Rönnegard, 2024; Silver, 2025). Egregopathy does not need to settle that metaphysical debate.

The practical test can remain behavioral and institutional. Does the organization detect harm that threatens outsiders but not itself? Can that detection alter goals rather than merely messaging? Can an internal critic stop a profitable action without first converting the objection into financial, legal, or reputational risk? Does the collective preserve evidence that might later incriminate it? Does it compensate victims before compulsion? Does it change metrics when the metrics create predictable damage? These are observable properties of governance.

The crucial distinction is between empathy as communication and empathy as control. A customer-service system can produce exquisitely compassionate language while being unable to change the policy that produced the complaint. A government can acknowledge suffering while preserving the rule that causes it. An AI system can generate language judged highly empathic without possessing human affect; recent work demonstrates both the impressive empathic quality of some LLM outputs and the fact that people value identical responses differently when they believe AI produced them (Abbasian et al., 2024; Rubin et al., 2025). None of this tells us whether the system "feels." It tells us that moral language is increasingly cheap.

INFERENCE As moral language becomes cheaper, costly correction becomes more informative. The moral Turing test therefore asks the institution to pay. A credible conscience should sometimes destroy an option the organization wanted: abandon a product, release damaging evidence, compensate outsiders, accept slower growth, surrender jurisdiction, stop a weapons program, close an unsafe facility, or allow a critic to remain inconveniently employed. The exact sacrifice varies by domain, but the logic is constant. If the organization's moral vocabulary expands while its feasible action set never changes, the vocabulary may be serving legitimacy rather than constraint.

This gives the reader a practical tool for the chapters ahead. Whenever an institution claims to have learned, ask what became impossible afterward. An apology is evidence about language. A new constraint is evidence about architecture.

The moral Turing test should therefore be expensive. A collective demonstrates conscience not when it can describe the right thing, but when it can surrender revenue, prestige, territory, speed, convenience, or power because the human cost is judged unacceptable. The price paid is the evidence.
