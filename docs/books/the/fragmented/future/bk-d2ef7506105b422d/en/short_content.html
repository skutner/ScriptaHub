A possibility, not a prediction

The book begins with a tension rather than a forecast. AI may reduce the cost of expertise, translation, planning, legal interpretation, and administration, allowing smaller organizations and regions to handle complexity that once demanded a large bureaucracy. The same technology may also give states, continental blocs, and platforms extraordinary powers of surveillance, coordination, persuasion, and control. Both tendencies can occur together. “Fragmentation” therefore does not mean inevitable secession, ethnic partition, or romantic village life. The preferred hypothesis is constitutional and controlled: strong cities and regions nested inside federations that retain common rights, defense, redistribution, strategic infrastructure, and authority over large externalities. The book carefully separates current observations, historical analogies, uncertain scenarios, and normative judgments. Its core question is not what will replace the nation-state, but how functions should be distributed within and around it. AI expands the feasible set; institutions and political choices determine which possibilities become durable.

Political scale as a changing bargain

History supplies mechanisms, not templates. City-states have supported participation, commerce, and institutional experimentation, but also oligarchy, exclusion, and war. Empires have built law, infrastructure, and long-distance order while extracting resources and suppressing difference. Federations distribute authority yet can entrench local injustice or suffer paralysis. The twentieth century rewarded national scale because mass armies, industrial supply chains, welfare systems, broadcast media, grids, and macroeconomic management favored standardization. AI may reverse only part of that settlement. A state can remain indispensable for security and redistribution without needing to control every school, permit, clinic, or development decision. City-regions are especially plausible because labour markets, universities, housing, transport, hospitals, and cultural life already operate at metropolitan scale. Yet they depend on surrounding territories for workers, food, water, energy, and logistics. Genuine autonomy consequently requires reciprocal compacts, realistic entry and exit, portable social insurance, housing access, and fiscal responsibility—not merely devolved administration or prosperity enclosed for incumbents.

Scarcity migrates into new bottlenecks

Cheap cognition is not the end of scarcity. AI can multiply code, explanations, designs, lessons, and plans while leaving energy, compute, chips, land, water, trusted attention, care, security, institutional legitimacy, and ecological stability constrained. Some bottlenecks become more valuable precisely because synthetic output is abundant. This shifts political economy toward the infrastructure stack. Thousands of nominally independent firms or municipalities may still depend on one model provider, identity layer, cloud platform, or agent interface. Legal plurality can conceal technical centralisation. The book therefore distinguishes returns that compensate useful risk and coordination from rents earned by controlling unavoidable interfaces. An “infrastructure state” could provide interoperable identity, payments, public compute, data exchanges, portability, and switching capacity while allowing plural services above them. But the same common layer could become a universal surveillance surface through mission creep. Purpose limitation, federated storage, independent authorization, cryptographic accountability, competition, and usable exit are constitutional protections, not optional technical refinements.

Elites, ambition, and meaning after work

AI may weaken the informational monopoly once attached to professional expertise without eliminating hierarchy. Influence may migrate toward control of institutions, networks, physical assets, trusted brands, compute, and certification. The book’s deliberately provocative idea of “revocable nobility” treats privilege as an advance against a social function: authority and resources remain defensible only while the function is performed and open to challenge. Polycentrism can multiply arenas for useful ambition, but local elites may be especially entrenched through dense family, property, media, and contracting networks. A low-work future raises another difficulty. Employment supplies status, routine, social contact, and a sense of being needed as well as income. If it weakens, competition may intensify in politics, religion, identity, culture, and synthetic communities. Institutions need many respected ladders—scientific, civic, artistic, educational, entrepreneurial, religious, and local—without allowing success on one to purchase the entire building. Care, mentoring, restoration, mediation, and public maintenance can become meaningful forms of contribution rather than coerced substitutes for jobs.

Computational empire and dark alignment

A computational empire need not begin with a dictator. It can emerge through individually reasonable integrations: health, tax, education, policing, transport, identity, and welfare systems share data and models to improve service. The danger lies in composition. Courts and regulators may remain legally independent while relying on the same vendor, evidence pipeline, or risk model. Appeals then reproduce the assumptions of the original decision. Agencies optimizing fraud, cost, delay, or order can gradually treat unconventional people as expensive anomalies without anyone consciously choosing oppression. Procurement can create this cognitive monoculture before legislation acknowledges it. The book calls the mechanism institutional dark alignment: an organization becomes exceptionally effective at its internal objective while human plurality remains outside the metric. Resilience therefore requires separation not only of formal powers but of models, data pipelines, vendors, and evaluative methods. Shared protocols with plural implementations, independent models, offline modes, auditable feedback variables, and preserved alternatives accept redundancy as the price of preventing one error or capture from becoming universal.

The democratic divide from the CEO-city

Because the book takes fragmentation seriously, it confronts neoreaction and the Dark Enlightenment rather than disguising their proximity. Both polycentric federalism and “Patchwork” imaginaries value smaller jurisdictions, clearer responsibility, experimentation, and exit. Their constitutional premises differ sharply. A corporate sovereign treats political authority more like ownership and residents more like customers; the proposed constitutional archipelago keeps citizenship prior to contract, rights above local bargains, courts outside local executives, fiscal solidarity across regions, and voice alongside exit. Mobility cannot discipline a ruler fairly when residents have homes, languages, families, illnesses, and unequal access to passports or capital. A private city might nevertheless deliver excellent services, which makes its risks more serious rather than less. Efficient optimization of property value, security, or desired-resident retention can exclude costly people and export harms. The democratic response should be competent institutions with clearer authority, faster building, accountable decisions, and genuine local experiments—not reverence for every inherited veto or the abandonment of equal citizenship.

A constitutional archipelago of resilience

The favored architecture refuses to grant permanent supremacy to any level. Layered sovereignty assigns functions according to scale, externalities, and local knowledge, then revises the assignment when evidence changes. Higher levels ordinarily protect constitutional rights, defense, macroeconomic stability, major redistribution, strategic infrastructure, and catastrophic-risk governance. Cities and regions can control many services, housing, transport, education, and cultural policies when they possess real revenue and competence. Equalization prevents successful regions from privatizing benefits produced by common markets, while fiscal accountability keeps local government from becoming a spending contractor for the center. Cosmolocalism adds global knowledge and selectively distributed physical capacity: open designs, AI-assisted adaptation, repair skills, local energy, and emergency production create resilience without pretending every region can manufacture semiconductors or medicines alone. Interoperability, model portability, appeals, anti-capture rules, and measurement across multiple outcomes allow systems to learn. The goal is not permanent decentralisation but a civilization capable of changing scale without destroying alternatives.

Why read the complete book

The complete book supplies the historical comparisons, contemporary political currents, and explicit qualifications that a compressed account cannot preserve. It tests the city-region against fiscal inequality, housing exclusion, rural dependence, cybersecurity, climate migration, environmental spillovers, platform control, and the secession of wealthy territories. Its treatment of Switzerland explains why strong cantons work only within dense federal law, coordination, insurance, and a distinctive history that cannot simply be copied. The full discussion of religion, synthetic charisma, AI companions, crisis leadership, neoreaction, and elite circulation makes the psychological stakes more concrete. Nine scenario histories then show how a benevolent technate, security empire, corporate archipelago, polycentric federation, and other regimes could emerge or transform into one another through ordinary defaults. Procurement rules, tax assignments, emergency statutes, data architecture, and appeal procedures prove more consequential than slogans. Reading those cases reveals why the preferred constitutional archipelago is a demanding hedge, not a prediction or an uncomplicated endorsement of smaller government.
