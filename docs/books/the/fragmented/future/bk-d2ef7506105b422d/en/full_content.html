PREFACE

This book begins from a possibility rather than a forecast. Artificial intelligence may alter the economic reasons for which large political organizations became attractive, while simultaneously giving those same organizations unprecedented capacities for surveillance, administration, prediction, persuasion, and coordination. If both changes occur together, the twenty-first century will face an unusual institutional problem. The technologies that make some forms of centralization less necessary may also make centralization more powerful. The result need not be the disappearance of states. It may instead be a struggle over the internal scale of states: whether political life remains organized around one dominant national center, becomes absorbed into continental or technological blocs, is delegated to city-regions, or fractures into public and private jurisdictions of very different kinds.

The word fragmentation is therefore dangerous and useful. It is dangerous because it can suggest ethnic partition, civil war, secession, gated enclaves, or the romantic claim that small communities are naturally virtuous. None of those meanings is assumed here. The hypothesis explored most sympathetically is a controlled and constitutional fragmentation of decision-making: strong cities and regions embedded in larger federations that retain common rights, defense, redistribution, strategic infrastructure, and mechanisms for handling externalities. The closest historical family is not the collapse of an empire into warlords but polycentric federalism, with Switzerland as one unusually durable reference point and Elinor and Vincent Ostrom as important theoretical guides [Ostrom, 2010; Ostrom, Tiebout & Warren, 1961]. Even that model is not treated as a blueprint. It is a clue.

The argument matters because AI changes scarcity. A great deal of political economy was built around scarce labor, scarce expertise, scarce administrative attention, scarce information, and the high cost of coordinating large groups. Generative and agentic systems can reduce some of those costs. But abundance in one layer does not produce abundance everywhere. Compute, energy, advanced chips, land in desirable places, trusted institutions, embodied care, physical security, cultural belonging, status, and the legitimate authority to decide remain scarce. Some may become more scarce precisely because machine intelligence becomes cheap. The central economic question is therefore not whether scarcity disappears, but where it migrates and who owns the new bottlenecks.

That change also destabilizes the social meaning of elites. Historically, elites did not merely extract. They also organized armies, financed infrastructure, preserved knowledge, administered law, mediated long-distance exchange, and absorbed risks that others could not. These functions never justified every privilege, and they often coexisted with exploitation. Yet a future in which machine systems cheapen expertise and management may expose privileges that were previously defended as payments for scarce competence. The likely result is not an egalitarian vacuum. Humans compete for status even when material goods are plentiful. New elites may arise around compute, energy, network control, cultural legitimacy, security, trust, and the capacity to define institutional goals. The problem becomes how to make elite status functional, contestable, and revocable rather than hereditary in fact even when it is not hereditary in law.

The psychological dimension is equally important. Religion, aristocracy, armies, parties, universities, corporations, and bureaucracies have all served as social machines for channeling unusually ambitious, dominant, charismatic, or status-seeking individuals. They gave such people arenas in which to compete and, at least sometimes, rules by which their ambition was constrained. If AI compresses traditional careers and weakens the role of work as a source of identity, competition for political and symbolic status may intensify. This does not imply that political leaders can be diagnosed from afar or that traits such as narcissism or Machiavellianism explain history. Political psychology is probabilistic and context-dependent [Grijalva et al., 2015; Nai & Maier, 2024]. The more defensible question is institutional: what kinds of systems reward prestige, what kinds reward domination, and how does AI lower the cost of scaling either strategy?

The book also examines a possibility that the earlier edition treated too implicitly: the Dark Enlightenment, or neoreaction. This is not simply another name for decentralization. In writings associated especially with Curtis Yarvin and Nick Land, the movement joins criticism of democratic governance to anti-egalitarian premises, concentrated sovereignty, corporate analogies for the state, and the idea that competing jurisdictions can discipline government through exit [Yarvin, 2007; Yarvin, 2008; Smith & Burrows, 2021]. Its interest for this book is analytical. It represents one possible answer to the same problem that motivates polycentric federalism: what should happen when democratic institutions appear slow while technology enables much faster management? The answers diverge sharply. A constitutional archipelago multiplies centers of public power under common rights. Neocameralism can multiply sovereign owners. Similar geometry does not imply similar politics.

The darker possibility is broader than any ideology. AI can become aligned with the survival and expansion of the institution that controls it, even when that institution is not aligned with the autonomy of the persons inside it. I call this institutional dark alignment. The phrase is deliberately distinguished from technical AI alignment. It does not assume machine intention. A ministry that rewards systems for reducing measured risk, a platform that rewards systems for retention, a political movement that rewards systems for loyalty, or a private city that rewards systems for asset value can all select increasingly effective mechanisms that optimize the principal's metric while degrading unmeasured human goods. A system may become more competent and less humane at the same time.

The method of the book is deliberately plural. History provides analogies but not laws. Anthropology provides models of hierarchy, ritual, coalition, and social scale but cannot turn humanity into one culture. Sociology shows how institutions reproduce status and legitimacy but does not predict a single post-work order. Psychology helps explain dominance, prestige, belonging, and threat but cannot diagnose civilizations. Economics clarifies incentives, rents, externalities, and public goods but cannot decide what a human life is for. Political theory distinguishes voice, exit, rights, sovereignty, and legitimacy but cannot make trade-offs disappear. The book therefore treats the future as a branching tree, not a destination.

Its slight preference for a polycentric, cosmolocal direction is consequently conditional. It rests on a resilience argument rather than on localist sentiment. When the future is deeply uncertain, multiple partially independent experiments can preserve options and limit correlated failure. When knowledge can travel globally while some material capacities remain local, the combination may produce both learning and continuity. Yet fragmentation becomes dangerous when externalities are global, when rich regions can secede from obligations, when local majorities can strip rights, when private owners become sovereign, or when military and AI races reward reckless jurisdictions. A resilient archipelago therefore needs a center with real capacity. The question is not how to abolish the center. It is how to prevent the center from becoming the only place where civilization can think.

METHODOLOGICAL NOTE

This is a work of disciplined speculation. It distinguishes four layers of claim. An empirical observation concerns evidence available now. A historical analogy identifies a mechanism that operated in a different setting. A scenario extrapolates mechanisms under explicit uncertainty. A normative judgment states which trade-offs appear preferable. These layers are intentionally kept separate. A historical precedent can be real without being transferable. A future can be plausible without being desirable. An ideology can identify a genuine problem while proposing an unacceptable solution.

The word state is also used at several levels. Nation-states remain the principal holders of coercive and fiscal authority, but the book discusses municipalities, metropolitan regions, provinces, federations, continental unions, transnational platforms, and global institutions as partially overlapping governance layers. The model favored in several chapters is therefore better described as layered sovereignty than as decentralization. Some functions should move downward, others upward. Subsidiarity is not the rule that the smallest unit always wins; it is the rule that a function should be located at the lowest level capable of handling its externalities, with the burden of proof on unnecessary upward concentration.

Historical comparisons are bounded. Europe and China are not controlled experiments; Rome and Switzerland are not prototypes; Greek poleis and modern metropolitan regions differ radically in population, technology, rights, and economic integration. Comparisons are used to isolate mechanisms such as jurisdictional competition, exit, standardization, military mobilization, information compression, or institutional redundancy. Where the mechanism depends on slavery, colonial extraction, ethnic homogeneity, premodern warfare, or geography that no longer applies, the analogy is explicitly weakened.

The language of elites is sociological rather than honorific. Complex societies generate unequal distributions of influence regardless of their official ideology. The phrase revocable nobility is a metaphor for privilege tied to function and accountability, not a proposal for hereditary ranks. Likewise, discussions of personality traits are not remote diagnoses of public figures. Constructs such as the Dark Triad have limited measurement validity, significant cultural dependence, and modest explanatory power compared with institutions and incentives. They are used to ask how systems select behavioral strategies, not to classify enemies.

The linked Wikipedia article on the Dark Enlightenment was used as a terminological and bibliographic map, not as an authority. The chapter on neoreaction triangulates it with Yarvin's archived primary texts, Smith and Burrows's peer-reviewed study of neoreactionary politics of exit, and critical work by Yuk Hui and Roger Burrows [Smith & Burrows, 2021; Hui, 2017; Hui, 2025]. Contemporary journalistic discussions are treated as evidence that the ideas have received renewed attention, not as proof that any current government is secretly governed by a single doctrine.

Finally, resilience is defined broadly. The survival of technological civilization is not satisfied by keeping data centers and factories running under permanent domination. A resilient civilization must preserve material continuity, error correction, plural sources of knowledge, the ability to contest authority, intergenerational cooperation, and a minimum of personal autonomy. These values sometimes conflict. No architecture eliminates tragic choices. The objective is to design institutions that can make serious mistakes without making them universal, permanent, or impossible to criticize.

CHAPTER 1

THE MAP OF POSSIBLE FUTURES

The central proposition of this book can be stated in one sentence: artificial intelligence may make smaller political units more administratively viable at the same time that it makes very large political units more powerful. That tension creates not one future but a field of futures. The first chapter maps that field before the rest of the book slows down to examine its historical, economic, sociological, psychological, and institutional mechanisms. The reader should therefore treat this chapter as the compressed version of the entire argument. Later chapters are not a sequence of new theses so much as attempts to test, qualify, and sometimes weaken what is asserted here.

The first possibility is the computational empire. It need not be a literal world empire and need not be authoritarian in its constitutional form. Its defining feature is vertical integration. Identity, taxation, benefits, health, education, security, infrastructure, and economic policy become connected through common data and AI systems. The attraction is immense. Administrative friction falls. Fraud can be detected quickly. Social programs become automatic. Planning improves. A state that previously needed millions of clerical decisions can run through a smaller number of high-level policy choices executed by agents. If the same infrastructure controls eligibility, risk assessment, appeals, and information, however, the system can become difficult to contest even while elections continue. The danger is not necessarily tyranny by a malicious ruler. It is institutional monoculture.

A second future is the sovereign platform. Here states remain on maps, but daily life is mediated by private ecosystems. One company may provide the personal agent, identity layer, payment system, work marketplace, education, health navigation, insurance, reputation, and communication. The platform becomes a functional sovereign because exit from it means leaving much of social and economic life. This future extends current platform economics rather than overthrowing it. AI strengthens the tendency because a deeply personalized agent becomes more valuable as it accumulates context, making portability technically and commercially difficult. Regulation can preserve competition, but only if identity, data, memory, and agency are separable from one vendor.

A third future is the augmented nation-state. This is probably the least dramatic and therefore one of the most plausible. Existing states absorb AI into public administration, industrial strategy, welfare, defense, taxation, education, and diplomacy. National identity may become more important as governments compete for chips, energy, talent, models, and supply chains. The result can be democratic, illiberal, or authoritarian depending on institutions. Techno-nationalism is compatible with social democracy, conservatism, developmentalism, or populism. Its core is not ideology but the belief that technological capacity has become part of sovereignty. The emerging literature on strategic digital sovereignty and techno-blocs already describes movement in this direction [Weymouth, 2025; Fratini et al., 2026].

A fourth future is the world of techno-blocs. No state is truly sovereign across the AI stack. Advanced chips, fabrication tools, cloud systems, models, energy, capital, and scientific talent are geographically distributed. States therefore form selective alliances with firms and other states. The world becomes less like one global internet and more like overlapping technological spheres. The logic resembles older military and trade blocs but with corporations embedded much more directly in strategic governance. This future can stabilize supply chains inside blocs while making the international order more hierarchical and brittle. It can also subordinate smaller states that formally retain sovereignty but lack meaningful control over their cognitive infrastructure.

A fifth future is a family of private jurisdictions, network states, charter cities, and experimental zones. These are not identical and should not be collapsed into one ideology. Their shared intuition is that communities can organize around rules and networks before or alongside territory, and that jurisdictional competition may produce better governance. In modest forms, the idea is already normal: special economic zones, metropolitan authorities, university towns, indigenous jurisdictions, and cross-border networks all create differentiated rules. AI lowers the administrative cost of such differentiation. The danger is that a jurisdiction can select its residents, externalize expensive obligations, and confuse a favorable clientele with superior institutions.

A sixth future is the Dark Enlightenment or neocameralist scenario. It must be separated from the previous family because it adds explicit political premises. In Yarvin's formalism and Patchwork, diffuse democratic sovereignty is replaced by more legible ownership and executive authority; in Land's Dark Enlightenment, neoreaction is joined to a more radical accelerationist imagination. The state becomes analogous to a firm and exit becomes a principal constraint on rulers [Yarvin, 2007; Yarvin, 2008; Smith & Burrows, 2021]. The appeal is the promise of competence, responsibility, and freedom from procedural paralysis. The cost is that citizenship can become customer status and rights can become contingent on a sovereign whose power is not derived from equal political voice. AI makes the scenario more technically plausible by allowing a small executive center to administer complex societies with fewer human intermediaries.

A seventh future is the polycentric federation. It keeps states and federations but moves meaningful authority toward metropolitan regions, provinces, municipalities, and functional communities. The center retains constitutional rights, defense, macroeconomic stabilization, redistribution, infrastructure standards, and control of risks that cannot be localized. Local units receive fiscal resources and room to experiment. The Swiss Confederation is a partial example because it combines strong cantons and communes with federal institutions, direct democracy, fiscal coordination, and shared citizenship. The Ostrom tradition generalizes the principle: complex problems can often be governed by multiple overlapping centers rather than by one hierarchy or a pure market [Ostrom, 2010]. The model is attractive under uncertainty because failure can remain local while learning travels.

An eighth future is cosmolocalism. It is economic before it is constitutional. Knowledge, software, design, scientific methods, and cultural artifacts circulate globally as commons or interoperable standards, while some production, repair, energy, food, fabrication, and services become more local. AI can make globally shared knowledge usable by small communities that previously lacked expert staff. The model is not autarky. Semiconductor fabs, frontier science, aerospace, and many medicines will continue to require enormous scale. Cosmolocalism is instead selective redundancy: globalize what is non-rival and localize enough material capacity that a broken supply chain does not become civilizational paralysis [Kostakis & Seitanidis, 2026].

A ninth future is uncontrolled fragmentation. The center loses fiscal or coercive capacity before institutions of federal cooperation are built. Rich regions, platforms, security providers, ideological communities, criminal networks, and local strongmen fill the vacuum. Some enclaves prosper. Others become peripheral. Formal mobility exists but is materially limited. This is the scenario most easily confused with the argument of the book. It is not the preferred endpoint of decentralization; it is one of its principal failure modes. The difference between polycentric federation and collapse is not the number of units. It is whether common rights, transfers, infrastructure, conflict resolution, and external security survive.

All nine futures depend on the migration of scarcity. Routine cognitive work is likely to become cheaper before energy, compute, trusted physical presence, legal responsibility, land in productive cities, and social legitimacy do. The International Labour Organization's exposure measures already imply that transformation of tasks may be broader than direct job elimination, while economic estimates differ substantially on the magnitude and distribution of productivity gains [ILO, 2025; Acemoglu, 2024]. The uncertainty itself matters. A political constitution designed for one assumed labor-market outcome may fail if AI is either much more powerful or much less powerful than expected. Institutions need to survive both abundance and disappointment.

The likely conflict over elites follows from the same mechanism. If management, coding, analysis, media production, and professional advice become less scarce, wealth and prestige may migrate toward ownership of scarce infrastructure and toward social positions that machines cannot automatically legitimize. Some current elites will defend their rents. New elites will claim competence in AI, energy, security, community leadership, or cultural interpretation. Peter Turchin's theory of elite overproduction is relevant as a scenario mechanism: if many aspirants possess resources and ambition but the number of high-status positions shrinks, counter-elites can intensify political conflict [Turchin, 2023]. AI can magnify this because a small team can now operate media, research, fundraising, and organization at a scale that once required institutions.

The social role of work is therefore as important as wages. Industrial societies made employment a source of time discipline, adult identity, prestige, friendship, political organization, and moral worth. If AI weakens this structure, people will not become psychologically neutral consumers. They will search for status, ritual, mission, belonging, and credible ways to be needed. Religion may revive in new forms. Political movements may become more cultic. Civic service, care, science, sport, craftsmanship, ecological restoration, local government, and community institutions may gain status. The dangerous assumption is that material abundance automatically produces liberal tolerance. Existential insecurity can decline while status competition remains intense.

The psychological question intersects with institutional scale. A single political center creates a very large prize. Highly ambitious actors have a strong reason to capture it, and losing coalitions have a strong reason to treat each election as existential. Multiple centers can distribute prestige and power, but they can also produce multiple small tyrannies. The solution cannot be fragmentation alone. It requires rights above local power, appeal to external courts, portability of identity and assets, separation of police from data monopolies, and limits on emergency authority. Exit is valuable only when people can actually exercise it; voice remains necessary because humans cannot move like liquid capital.

The strongest argument for some centralization is externality. Nuclear weapons, frontier AI, pandemics, climate systems, financial contagion, space infrastructure, and major wars do not respect municipal boundaries. A city-region that gains from risky AI research while exporting the catastrophic tail risk to everyone else should not have a veto-free right to experiment. The same logic applies to tax competition that erodes the common fiscal base. Polycentrism is not the absence of hierarchy. It is a hierarchy whose layers are narrower in purpose and whose powers can be challenged from multiple directions.

The strongest argument for decentralization is correlated error. A single model, procurement stack, ideology, fiscal rule, or administrative doctrine can fail everywhere at once. Complex systems theory and resilience research warn against optimization that removes redundancy [Holling, 1973; Perrow, 1984; Woods, 2018]. AI increases this concern because one successful system can be copied almost instantly. What appears as efficient standardization can turn into epistemic monoculture. A federation that allows several compatible implementations of a public function may pay more in normal times and lose less in abnormal times.

The political currents visible in 2026 suggest that no pure model will win. V-Dem and Freedom House document continuing democratic stress in many countries, while governments simultaneously expand digital administration and industrial policy [V-Dem, 2026; Freedom House, 2026; OECD, 2026a]. The United Nations has begun a new Global Dialogue on AI Governance, while national strategies emphasize sovereignty and competition [United Nations, 2026]. Techno-optimism, national conservatism, social-democratic redistribution, post-growth politics, localism, network-state imaginaries, and neoreaction circulate simultaneously. The most likely order is hybrid: more capable states, stronger technology blocs, powerful platforms, selective local autonomy, and experiments at the margins.

The book's conditional preference can now be stated precisely. For civilizational resilience, a polycentric federation combined with cosmolocal capabilities appears attractive when four conditions hold: rights remain portable and superior to local contracts; the center retains enough capacity to manage catastrophic externalities and territorial redistribution; infrastructure is interoperable rather than vertically monopolized; and local autonomy is fiscal and operational rather than ceremonial. If those conditions fail, fragmentation can become secession of the rich, corporate sovereignty, or collapse. If centralization has no counterweights, integration can become computational empire. The objective is not to choose one scale forever. It is to build a civilization capable of changing scale without destroying itself.

CHAPTER 2

THE AI SHOCK IS A CHANGE IN THE COST OF COGNITION

Artificial intelligence is often discussed as if its political effects followed directly from job displacement. That is too narrow. The more consequential transformation may be a decline in the cost of cognition itself. Modern organizations grew partly because coordination, accounting, legal interpretation, planning, translation, and management were expensive. Large firms and bureaucracies could afford specialist layers that small organizations could not. When agents perform a significant share of those functions, the minimum efficient scale of administration may fall. A municipality that once depended on a national ministry for expertise could obtain competent analysis locally. A small company could operate with capabilities previously available only to a multinational. A scientific community could maintain sophisticated infrastructure without a large permanent bureaucracy.

This does not imply the end of economies of scale. AI itself is produced through highly concentrated infrastructures. Frontier systems require chips, data centers, energy, research teams, capital, and supply chains whose scale is enormous. OECD work on AI markets in 2026 emphasizes competition risks across this stack, while the Stanford AI Index documents continued concentration of frontier development [OECD, 2026c; Stanford HAI, 2026]. The paradox is therefore structural: intelligence can be consumed at small scale while the means of producing frontier intelligence remain concentrated. Political decentralization built on a single external cognitive supplier may be decentralization only on paper.

Labor effects are similarly ambiguous. Exposure to generative AI does not equal automation, and automation does not equal unemployment. Technologies can substitute for tasks, complement workers, create new demand, and redistribute bargaining power. Research in customer support and professional tasks already shows substantial productivity effects, especially for less experienced workers, but the frontier is jagged and task-specific [Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2023; Dell'Acqua et al., 2023]. Macro estimates remain uncertain. Acemoglu's cautious estimates differ from more transformative scenarios, and the IMF's 2026 scenario exercise explicitly treats AI as a macro-critical transition with wide uncertainty [Acemoglu, 2024; IMF, 2026]. The institutional problem is to remain viable across these branches.

If AI mostly complements skilled labor, large metropolitan economies may become even more dominant because talent and capital cluster around the best tools. If AI substitutes for a large range of white-collar work, the political economy changes more sharply. Tax bases tied to wages become fragile. Professional identity weakens. The relative return to owning compute, energy, intellectual property, distribution, or land may rise. Regions that host data centers or power generation can become strategic in ways that resemble resource provinces, though the analogy is incomplete because digital capital is more mobile and politically networked than oil wells.

A state can respond through compensation, universal services, broader ownership of productive assets, protection of incumbent work, or combinations of these. Their relative merit depends on how powerful AI becomes. Compensation is sufficient when disruption is moderate. If labor's share of income falls structurally, ownership becomes more important than transfers because the fiscal system otherwise redistributes after concentration rather than shaping it. Public or pension funds holding diversified capital can be understood as insurance against a world in which citizens increasingly consume the returns of automation but do not directly earn them through wages.

The same logic applies to municipalities. Cities depend heavily on property, consumption, wage, and business taxes in different proportions. AI can change all four. A region with valuable land may collect increasing rents even when employment falls. A region dependent on commuter income may suffer. Automated remote work can weaken the link between residence and production. These fiscal asymmetries make a purely local solution impossible. A federation of strong regions requires equalization mechanisms, or prosperous nodes will accumulate capacity while peripheral areas lose both people and tax revenue.

AI also changes the state's informational justification. Central governments historically benefited from specialized statistics, planning departments, legal expertise, and administrative memory. If those capacities become cheaper, local units can become more competent. But the center gains the same technologies. It can see farther into local systems, simulate policy, automate compliance, and integrate databases. Whether AI decentralizes therefore depends less on the technology than on ownership, standards, procurement, constitutional competence, and the right to run independent models.

The political importance of administrative scale becomes clearer if we imagine two municipalities with equal populations but different access to expertise. In the twentieth century the richer municipality could hire planners, engineers, lawyers, statisticians, and grant specialists while the poorer one depended on central agencies. An AI layer can narrow that gap, but only if the poorer municipality has data, compute, procurement competence, and the legal authority to use the advice. Cheap cognition is not the same as cheap institutional capacity. It can amplify capable local governments before it rescues weak ones.

The same asymmetry applies to firms and civil society. A cooperative, union, clinic, newspaper, or research nonprofit can use agents to perform functions that once required professional departments. That may thicken the institutional ecology between person and state, reversing some of the consolidation produced by twentieth-century economies of scale. But if all of these organizations rely on the same platform, a nominally plural society can still become structurally centralized. The relevant unit of concentration is increasingly the infrastructure stack rather than the legal organization.

For this reason, the AI shock should be treated as a constitutional shock even before its employment effects are known. It changes who can plausibly administer complexity. Political systems built on the assumption that only the center can afford competence may become unnecessarily centralized. Systems built on the assumption that local units can always manage may discover that frontier infrastructure has created new dependencies. The appropriate response is experimental federalism with measurement, not a pre-committed doctrine of either centralization or devolution.

CHAPTER 3

WHAT HISTORY ACTUALLY TEACHES ABOUT POLITICAL SCALE

Political scale has never moved in one direction. Human history alternates between aggregation and fragmentation because different problems reward different sizes. Small communities often have dense social knowledge and rapid accountability. Large states can mobilize armies, standardize law, pool risk, build infrastructure, and control long-distance trade. Empires solve some coordination problems by making them internal; they create others by increasing distance between rulers and local knowledge. City-states can innovate under competition; they can also spend generations at war. Any argument that one scale is naturally superior is contradicted by the diversity of successful and failed societies.

The Greek polis is an obvious but dangerous analogy. Political participation, military obligation, religion, and civic identity were closely linked, and competition among poleis generated institutional diversity. Yet the system rested on social structures, slavery, gender exclusion, and warfare that make direct imitation meaningless. What transfers is not the polis itself but a mechanism: neighboring jurisdictions allowed practices, alliances, and people to move, making political organization partly competitive. The mechanism helped produce experimentation but also prevented collective security when coordination failed.

The Roman experience provides the reverse lesson. Rome created a vast infrastructure of law, military power, roads, taxation, and citizenship. Scale enabled extraordinary integration and security across large territories. It also created fiscal and military burdens whose management became increasingly complex. Joseph Tainter's account of diminishing returns to complexity remains controversial as a general theory, but it captures a real institutional possibility: additional layers can solve problems while generating maintenance costs that later become hard to bear [Tainter, 1988]. AI could lower some of those costs, making large systems more sustainable rather than less.

Medieval and early modern Europe are frequently invoked to argue that political fragmentation encouraged innovation. Competition among courts, cities, churches, universities, and states gave scholars, merchants, and dissidents alternative patrons and destinations. Joel Mokyr and Walter Scheidel emphasize versions of this mechanism [Mokyr, 2016; Scheidel, 2019]. Yet the comparison is easily overstated. Fragmented Europe also produced devastating wars, religious persecution, colonization, and predatory states. Innovation cannot be attributed to fragmentation alone. Geography, access to trade, coal, institutions of science, colonial resources, and historical contingency mattered.

China complicates the story further. Long periods of imperial unification supported standardized administration, large infrastructure, examination systems, and enormous internal markets. Periods of division also existed and could be highly innovative. Comparing China with Europe is therefore useful only when the causal question is narrow. Multiple jurisdictions can reduce the cost of exit for ideas and people; unified empires can reduce transaction costs and support scale. Neither mechanism determines the whole civilizational outcome. Pomeranz's work on the Great Divergence is a reminder that simple cultural explanations are insufficient [Pomeranz, 2000].

The Islamic world, Indian polities, steppe empires, maritime Southeast Asia, the Hanseatic League, Venice, the Dutch Republic, and indigenous confederacies add further combinations. Some were networks with weak centers. Others were hierarchical but allowed local law. Some prospered through trade while externalizing violence to colonies or frontiers. The recurrent pattern is nested authority: households, clans, cities, guilds, religious jurisdictions, provinces, empires, and trade networks coexist. The modern sovereign nation-state simplified this picture legally without eliminating it socially.

AI may restore the practical importance of nested authority because coordination across layers becomes cheaper. The question is whether the layers remain mutually constraining or become a stack controlled by one infrastructure owner. Historical polycentricity was often messy because information was slow. Digital polycentricity can be fast, but speed creates a new risk: apparent diversity resting on identical software and identical models. The lesson of history is therefore not that fragmentation caused freedom. It is that plurality of power can create escape routes and experiments, provided it does not eliminate collective defense or reproduce domination locally.

The early modern Dutch Republic illustrates another ambiguity. Its provinces and cities possessed substantial autonomy, merchant networks were powerful, and the polity generated exceptional commercial and scientific dynamism. Yet its prosperity was entangled with colonial violence, slavery, and overseas extraction. Internal polycentricity does not imply benign external relations. A future archipelago could distribute authority among its members while exploiting territories or populations outside the privileged network. Any resilience argument must therefore include the boundary of solidarity, not merely the distribution of power inside the core.

The United States offers a modern example of federal scale changing over time. State autonomy was extensive, but civil war, industrialization, national markets, civil rights, social insurance, and military mobilization shifted important powers upward. Some centralization corrected profound local injustice; some created bureaucratic distance. The history warns against treating the original constitutional allocation as sacred. Federalism is an evolving bargain shaped by technology, war, mobility, and moral conflict.

The twentieth century generally rewarded larger administrative units because mass armies, national grids, welfare systems, broadcast media, industrial supply chains, and macroeconomic management favored standardization. Nuclear weapons and superpower rivalry intensified the logic. AI may reverse only part of it. The state can still be the necessary scale for security while no longer being the necessary scale for every school, permit, clinic, or economic-development decision. The future may therefore be not a return to premodern fragmentation but a technologically enabled redistribution of functions inside large states.

CHAPTER 4

THE RETURN OF THE CITY-REGION

The city is becoming politically interesting again because economic life is increasingly metropolitan while many constitutional systems remain organized around boundaries drawn for older settlement patterns. Labor markets, universities, hospitals, transport, housing, logistics, energy, culture, and innovation often operate at the scale of a city-region rather than a municipality or nation. The governance problem is familiar: the functional city crosses many local borders, while the legal city is only one unit among them. OECD work on inter-municipal cooperation in 2026 reflects the practical importance of shared services without erasing local identity [OECD, 2026b].

A city-region has several advantages as a political unit. Residents can observe outcomes more directly than they can in a national bureaucracy. Infrastructure is concrete. Housing exists or does not; trains run or do not; water fails or does not. Local political competition can therefore be more empirical. Metropolitan economies also possess enough scale to maintain universities, specialized hospitals, cultural institutions, and sophisticated administration. AI may lower the threshold further by providing legal, planning, translation, and analytical capacity to regions that previously lacked it.

Yet the city is not naturally democratic, inclusive, or resilient. Landowners can dominate. Housing scarcity can turn local democracy into a mechanism for insiders to exclude newcomers. Wealthy municipalities can resist metropolitan redistribution. Private developers can acquire quasi-governmental power. A city may improve its statistics by exporting poverty or refusing costly residents. Local knowledge is valuable, but local interests can be narrow. This is why city-regional autonomy needs external constitutional limits and fiscal equalization.

The appropriate unit also varies by function. Wastewater may require a watershed authority. Transit may need a metropolitan region. Schools may remain municipal with national funding. Electricity requires grids larger than cities. Defense is national or federal. AI safety may require international coordination. Polycentric governance is not a map with one ideal boundary; it is an architecture in which several boundaries overlap. Complexity is a cost, but a single boundary that mismatches every problem is also costly.

Direct participation can improve at smaller scales, but participation itself is scarce. Citizens cannot attend constant meetings about technical systems. AI can help by summarizing proposals, simulating impacts, translating, and enabling deliberation, yet it can also create synthetic consensus. If the same official model writes the proposal, summarizes criticism, predicts impacts, and recommends the final choice, participation becomes ceremonial. Local democracy in the AI era therefore requires epistemic separation: opposition, media, civil society, universities, and courts need independent cognitive tools.

The strongest version of the city-region model is consequently neither municipal romanticism nor corporate urbanism. It is metropolitan public power inside a federation. The region has its own revenue, planning authority, service capacity, and political institutions. The federation guarantees rights, mobility, competition, and transfers. Neighboring regions share infrastructure through compacts. The arrangement resembles federalism more than secession. Its purpose is to place ordinary decisions nearer to observable consequences while keeping catastrophic externalities and universal obligations above the locality.

Historical free cities and trading cities show why urban autonomy can be productive and predatory at once. Lübeck, Venice, Genoa, Amsterdam, and other commercial centers developed sophisticated legal and financial institutions because trade rewarded reliability. They also defended oligarchic interests, restricted citizenship, and used coercion beyond their walls. The modern city-region inherits the first mechanism without escaping the second. A city that competes globally may become very responsive to investors and very unresponsive to residents with low mobility.

The rural relationship is especially important. Metropolitan autonomy can look like political realism from inside the city and abandonment from outside it. Cities depend on water, food, energy, logistics, ecosystems, and labor drawn from larger territories. If tax and policy autonomy allows the metropolis to capture value while peripheral regions bear environmental or demographic costs, the model becomes extractive. City-regions therefore need reciprocal compacts with surrounding territories and national equalization that recognizes functional interdependence.

Migration rights also determine whether city competition is meaningful. A successful region that can exclude newcomers through zoning, licensing, or expensive housing does not face the same discipline as a market firm. Entry is as important as exit. A federal right of internal mobility, portable social insurance, and housing supply can prevent local autonomy from becoming a club good reserved for incumbents. The city-region becomes a genuine experiment only if people can realistically join as well as leave.

CHAPTER 5

SCARCITY DOES NOT END; IT MOVES

Scarcity does not disappear when intelligence becomes cheap. It changes shape. This is one of the most important corrections to popular images of an AI economy. Information has already become abundant while attention became more valuable. Digital copying made content cheap while reputation, discovery, and trusted curation remained scarce. AI extends the pattern. It can produce code, text, analysis, designs, lessons, and plans at low marginal cost, but someone must still decide which outputs are correct, safe, legitimate, and worth acting on.

Energy is an obvious bottleneck. Data centers convert electrical and physical infrastructure into cognitive services. The IEA's 2025 and 2026 work on energy and AI emphasizes both rising electricity demand from data centers and AI's potential to improve energy systems [IEA, 2025; IEA, 2026]. A society with abundant models but constrained grids may experience political conflicts over power allocation. Regions with cheap, reliable energy can gain bargaining power. The politics of AI may therefore resemble the politics of industrial infrastructure more than the frictionless internet imagined in earlier digital theory.

Compute and chips form another scarcity. Their production depends on specialized equipment, fabs, materials, design ecosystems, and geopolitical relationships. A few firms and countries occupy critical positions. That concentration creates rents and strategic vulnerability. Even if open-weight models spread widely, the ability to train or run them at scale can remain concentrated. Digital sovereignty consequently becomes partly physical sovereignty over energy, cooling, chips, networks, and cloud capacity.

Land and embodied presence also remain scarce. If remote cognitive labor becomes easier, highly desirable cities may become more, not less, expensive because people choose them for social and cultural reasons. Care, trust, craftsmanship, performance, hospitality, physical security, and intimate relationships cannot be duplicated like files. AI can assist them but not eliminate the human preference for real bodies and shared places. A post-work economy may shift value toward experiences and locations whose supply is inherently limited.

Legitimacy is perhaps the most important political scarcity. A model can generate ten competent policy proposals, but it cannot mechanically determine which sacrifices are fair. When goals conflict, someone must possess a recognized right to decide. That right depends on law, procedure, identity, trust, performance, or some combination. AI may make policy analysis cheap while making legitimate judgment more visible as the true bottleneck. This is why a purely technocratic future is unstable: intelligence can reduce disagreement about means without eliminating disagreement about ends.

Status and meaning are also positional. If every person has access to expert assistance, expertise loses some of its exclusivity. Humans can create new status hierarchies around authenticity, service, risk, beauty, local leadership, contribution to commons, athletic performance, religious commitment, or control of scarce infrastructure. Such hierarchies are not necessarily harmful. Societies need ways to recognize contribution. The danger arises when status becomes detached from social function and converts into durable political or economic rent.

The institutional response should therefore focus on bottlenecks rather than on the fantasy of universal abundance. Competition policy matters where AI markets concentrate. Public ownership or regulated access may matter for infrastructure. Land policy matters in successful city-regions. Portability matters for identity and agents. Open standards matter for knowledge. Social policy must address care and participation. The political economy of AI will be determined less by the average cost of intelligence than by the ownership and governance of what intelligence cannot cheaply reproduce.

Security is another scarcity that can intensify with abundance. When software and autonomous capabilities diffuse, the cost of attack can fall alongside the cost of defense. Reliable identity, cybersecurity, physical protection, and trusted institutions become valuable precisely because destructive cognition is cheap. A society rich in synthetic knowledge can still be poor in trustworthy channels. This creates a temptation to centralize verification, which can protect against fraud while creating a monopoly over what counts as authentic.

Water, climate stability, and ecosystem services remain material constraints that AI can optimize but not repeal. Better forecasting can allocate scarce resources more efficiently, yet efficiency does not create rainfall or habitable land. Climate migration may increase the value of politically stable and environmentally resilient regions. The future map of city-regions could therefore be shaped as much by water and heat as by software. Digital abundance does not make geography disappear; it can make high-quality geography more positional.

Attention completes the picture. When every organization and individual can generate persuasive media at negligible cost, the scarce object is credible attention. Institutions able to filter, certify, and convene become more powerful. Universities, trusted brands, civic institutions, and social networks may gain or lose status depending on whether they can resist synthetic overload. The politics of abundance thus repeatedly recreates gatekeepers. The normative task is not to abolish gatekeeping but to make it plural, contestable, and accountable.

CHAPTER 6

FROM THE EXTRACTIVE STATE TO THE INFRASTRUCTURE STATE

The phrase extractive system is often used too broadly. Markets, firms, taxes, and profit are not identical to extraction. Private capital finances risk, discovers demand, coordinates investment, and can discipline failed projects. States supply law, insurance, infrastructure, security, public goods, and redistribution. The more precise question is which returns compensate socially useful functions and which persist because an actor controls a bottleneck. AI matters because it can reduce the cost of some functions while increasing the value of bottleneck ownership.

Industrial capitalism rewarded owners partly because capital goods were scarce and coordination was difficult. Managerial capitalism rewarded layers of administration because large organizations required information processing. Platform capitalism added rents from networks, data, attention, and ecosystem lock-in. Agentic AI can create a new layer: cognitive infrastructure. If one firm controls the agent through which a person works, shops, learns, negotiates, and communicates, it can extract value at the interface even when the underlying intelligence is cheap.

States can become more extractive as well. A highly integrated digital administration can replace visible taxation with a dense field of behavioral conditions, automated fines, licensing, risk scores, and data exchange. It can make compliance nearly frictionless for the state while transferring the cost of error to the citizen. Administrative efficiency is therefore not equivalent to public value. The critical design question is whether the citizen has independent representation, appeal, and control over the data and models used against them.

A more attractive alternative is the infrastructure state. Its legitimacy comes less from managing every activity and more from making many forms of activity possible. It supplies interoperable identity, payments, legal standards, education, transport, energy, public compute, safety institutions, and social insurance while allowing firms, cooperatives, regions, and communities to build on top. This resembles the way successful states historically supported roads, standards, money, research, and public health without producing every private good.

AI strengthens the case because public infrastructure can reduce dependence on any single platform. A citizen should be able to take identity, records, agent memory, credentials, and entitlements to another provider. A municipality should be able to replace an AI vendor without rewriting its entire administration. Open standards and public reference implementations can make markets more competitive precisely by reducing switching costs. Public capacity here is not the enemy of decentralization; it is what makes meaningful exit possible.

The fiscal system must also adapt. If wage income becomes a smaller share of national income, taxation tied primarily to labor becomes unstable. Consumption taxes can be regressive, while corporate profits can move across borders. Land, resource rents, capital income, and returns to public investment become more important. One plausible model is to treat part of AI infrastructure as a strategic asset held by sovereign, regional, pension, or civic funds so that citizens receive capital returns rather than relying only on redistribution after the fact.

The infrastructure state is not automatically liberal. The same common systems that enable portability can enable surveillance. A public identity layer can protect citizens from platforms or become a universal tracking device. The difference lies in constitutional separation, purpose limitation, multiple implementations, independent audit, and the possibility of offline or degraded modes. Infrastructure should be powerful enough to support society and constrained enough not to become society's owner.

The modern welfare state itself offers a useful analogy. It centralized insurance against unemployment, illness, disability, and old age because local charity and private insurance could not reliably pool large risks. That centralization was not merely bureaucratic expansion; it created social citizenship. A future infrastructure state should preserve this lesson. Devolving service delivery does not require devolving the guarantee. A region can administer care differently while the federation guarantees that a person's basic entitlement survives migration or local fiscal distress.

Public utilities provide another model. Electricity, water, transport, and telecommunications have historically occupied mixed regimes of public ownership, private provision, regulated monopoly, and competitive markets. AI infrastructure may evolve similarly. There is no reason to assume that one ownership form fits models, compute, identity, data exchanges, and agents. The key is to identify where natural-monopoly characteristics, network effects, or security externalities justify public obligations and where competition remains feasible.

The main danger of the infrastructure-state idea is mission creep. Once the state builds a shared layer, every agency has an incentive to add fields, integrate datasets, and reuse identity for new purposes. A system designed to make switching easy can become the most convenient surveillance architecture ever created. Purpose limitation, federated storage, cryptographic accountability, independent authorization, and legal remedies are therefore not technical decorations. They are what separates enabling infrastructure from a universal control surface.

CHAPTER 7

ELITES AFTER EXPERTISE: THE IDEA OF REVOCABLE NOBILITY

Every society has elites even when it denies the word. Unequal influence can derive from wealth, military command, education, religious authority, reputation, technical skill, family networks, control of organizations, or proximity to the state. Classical elite theorists such as Pareto, Mosca, and Michels sometimes treated this persistence as nearly inevitable. More recent work is less deterministic, but the empirical observation remains: large organizations generate leadership strata and information asymmetries [Michels, 1915; Diefenbach, 2019]. The relevant question is not whether elites exist but what functions justify their privileges and how easily they can be replaced.

Historical elites often performed genuine coordination roles. Warrior aristocracies organized defense, though they could become predatory. Priestly classes preserved texts and ritual, though they could monopolize legitimacy. Merchant elites financed trade and cities, though they often captured political institutions. Bureaucratic elites created administrative continuity, though they could become self-protective. Scientific and professional elites accumulated expertise that ordinary citizens could not cheaply verify. None of these functions absolves exploitation; it explains why hierarchy repeatedly reappeared.

AI attacks one justification especially strongly: scarcity of codified expertise. If high-quality legal, financial, technical, managerial, and scientific assistance becomes widely available, credentials no longer guarantee the same informational monopoly. This can democratize capability. It can also make elite competition harsher because status shifts from knowledge itself to control of institutions, networks, trusted brands, physical assets, and the authority to certify which machine outputs count.

The phrase revocable nobility names one possible response. It is intentionally provocative and should not be read literally. It proposes no hereditary title and no legal superiority. It begins from the recognition that societies advance resources, attention, autonomy, and authority to people in important roles. Those privileges should be understood as an advance against a function. When the function disappears, the privilege should be reversible. The more dangerous elite is not the visible officeholder but the informal actor whose influence is enormous and whose obligations are undefined.

Smaller political units can make elite performance more legible. A mayor, regional utility, university, or civic fund can be evaluated against visible outcomes. Yet local elites can be more entrenched because social networks are dense. Families, developers, media, and contractors may form durable coalitions. Historical localism frequently coexisted with oligarchy. Devolution without anti-capture institutions can simply replace a distant bureaucracy with a familiar oligarchy.

The solution is not permanent hostility to elites, because societies need people willing to assume responsibility and build difficult institutions. It is to multiply pathways to prestige while limiting accumulation of incompatible powers. The same person should not control data, policing, procurement, media, and the appeals process. Public honors can reward scientific openness, care, restoration, mediation, teaching, and civic construction rather than only wealth or political victory. AI can make evaluation richer, but the evaluation system itself must be plural.

A civilization facing rapid technological change also needs counter-elites. Institutions decay when incumbents can block all challengers. The task is therefore to make elite circulation peaceful and functional. Elections are one mechanism; professional competition, independent media, universities, entrepreneurial entry, regional autonomy, and open-source communities are others. A polycentric order can create more places for ambitious people to build without requiring them to conquer the national center. Its success depends on preventing those local successes from hardening into hereditary control.

Different civilizations have experimented with methods for converting inherited advantage into conditional office. Imperial examinations in China attempted, imperfectly, to recruit bureaucratic talent through learning rather than pure heredity. Republican Venice built elaborate procedures to prevent a single family from monopolizing the state while preserving an oligarchic class. Modern professional licensing and civil-service examinations similarly try to make authority depend on demonstrated competence. Each system eventually generated its own rents. The recurring lesson is that meritocratic filters become privileges unless entry and evaluation remain open to revision.

AI creates a special problem because it can make elite performance hard to attribute. A minister may look competent because an excellent model advises the office. A founder may appear visionary because the firm's agents search an enormous design space. A scientist may publish rapidly because machine collaborators perform much of the cognitive labor. This does not make achievement unreal, but it changes what society should reward. Choosing objectives, assuming responsibility, building trust, creating institutions, and sharing gains may become more important than personally performing every technical step.

Elite circulation also depends on the financing of challengers. If the owners of compute, media, land, and political infrastructure can fund only people who preserve the current order, formal elections do not guarantee circulation. A resilient system needs multiple capital pools: private venture, public research, civic funds, philanthropic institutions, regional investment, and open commons. Polycentric finance is as important as polycentric government because political and intellectual alternatives require resources before they can become credible.

CHAPTER 8

THE PSYCHOLOGY OF POWER IN A LOW-WORK WORLD

Political institutions are machines for managing ambition as much as mechanisms for making collective decisions. Anthropological evidence suggests that humans are capable of both hierarchy and coalitions against would-be dominators [Boehm, 1999]. Social psychology distinguishes two broad routes to status: prestige, granted to people considered competent or worthy of imitation, and dominance, produced through control, intimidation, or the ability to impose costs [Henrich & Gil-White, 2001; Cheng et al., 2013]. Real leaders combine both.

Modern institutions channel these strategies differently. Science rewards prestige in principle, though status competition can become vicious. Armies formalize hierarchy and duty. Markets reward value creation but can reward monopoly. Electoral politics rewards coalition and persuasion but can reward outrage and charismatic simplification. Religious organizations can discipline leaders through doctrine and office or elevate charismatic authority beyond correction. AI does not create these motives; it changes their scale.

Constructs such as narcissism, Machiavellianism, and subclinical psychopathy are relevant only with strong cautions. Meta-analyses associate some dark traits with manipulative or counterproductive behavior and show a nonlinear relationship between narcissism and leadership [O'Boyle et al., 2012; Grijalva et al., 2015]. Political research finds differences in perceived personality between autocratic and non-autocratic leaders, but causal interpretation is difficult [Nai & Toros, 2020; Nai & Maier, 2024]. Institutions select leaders, leaders reshape institutions, and observers rate personalities through partisan filters. Remote diagnosis is not a serious method.

AI matters because it gives small groups capabilities once available only to organizations. A charismatic political entrepreneur can generate media, analyze audiences, personalize messages, manage volunteers, raise funds, and simulate strategies with a tiny staff. A constructive civic entrepreneur can do the same for mediation, local services, or scientific collaboration. The reduction in organizational cost amplifies variance. It makes unusually competent or unusually destructive individuals more consequential.

A post-work or low-work society may intensify symbolic competition. Employment currently distributes not only income but rank, routine, social contact, and a sense of being needed. If those structures weaken, people may seek arenas where status remains scarce. Politics, religion, cultural conflict, sport, art, community leadership, gaming, and online identity can absorb that energy. A society that solves material poverty but offers no credible paths to contribution may remain politically unstable.

Centralization increases the prize. Capturing a single center that controls education, media regulation, taxation, public AI, security, and welfare becomes extraordinarily valuable. Polycentrism can reduce the prize by distributing domains and territories. But it also creates many smaller centers that charismatic actors can capture. The appropriate response is not to assume that local leaders are nicer; it is to create cross-level appeal, term limits where appropriate, separation of powers, transparent procurement, whistleblower protection, and a rights floor that local popularity cannot remove.

The deepest psychological argument for federalism is therefore modest. Humans are not reliably wise enough to deserve a single irreversible political prize. Nor are they reliably cooperative enough to live without common institutions. A resilient constitution assumes ambitious people will exist, gives them many socially useful arenas, and ensures that success in one arena does not automatically grant control over all the others.

Crisis changes the selection environment. Groups often prefer decisive leaders under perceived threat, and executives acquire powers that would be unacceptable in normal times. This can be rational when time is scarce. The institutional danger is not strong crisis leadership by itself but the conversion of emergency selection into permanent political style. AI can continuously generate risk forecasts, making it easier to justify a chronic state of exception. A resilient constitution therefore needs objective expiry rules and independent institutions capable of disputing the risk model itself.

Synthetic political presence adds another layer. A leader can maintain millions of personalized conversations through agents that imitate tone, remember constituents, and adapt arguments. Charisma, once limited by physical attention, can become a software service. This can make representatives more accessible, but it can also make the boundary between person and organization disappear. The future may require disclosure rules for political agents and public archives of materially different promises made to different groups.

Status pluralism is a neglected constitutional resource. If one hierarchy dominates society, ambitious people rationally concentrate on it. A civilization with respected scientific, civic, artistic, entrepreneurial, athletic, religious, educational, and local-political hierarchies distributes competition. None must be equal in prestige, but none should automatically convert success into control of all others. Polycentrism therefore has a cultural analogue: many ladders, with barriers against turning one ladder into the building itself.

CHAPTER 9

RELIGION, IDENTITY, STATUS, AND THE SEARCH FOR MEANING

Work, religion, and politics have all served as technologies of meaning. They organize calendars, identities, obligations, rituals, friendships, sacrifice, and explanations of suffering. Industrial modernity did not abolish these functions; it redistributed them. Employment became a major source of adult identity, the nation absorbed sacred symbolism, and political ideologies supplied narratives of progress or restoration. If AI weakens work as the central institution of everyday life, the displaced psychological functions will not disappear.

Religion may therefore become more important even in technologically advanced societies, but not necessarily through a simple return to old institutions. AI can support personalized theology, ritual communities, historical reconstruction, translation of sacred texts, and distributed education. It can also produce synthetic prophets, charismatic avatars, automated apologetics, or systems optimized for retention and loyalty. The anthropology of costly ritual suggests why communities with demanding practices can generate trust and durability [Sosis & Bressler, 2003; Whitehouse, 2004]. AI can reduce the cost of doctrine while leaving the social value of shared sacrifice intact.

Political communities can behave similarly. Parties already use symbols, founding narratives, heroes, traitors, ceremonies, and moral boundaries. Personalized AI can make political communication less like mass broadcasting and more like pastoral care, with every supporter receiving a tailored narrative. This can deepen participation or create fragmented realities. A movement no longer needs one coherent manifesto if its agent can generate compatible but different explanations for each constituency.

This is a clear environment for institutional dark alignment. A movement that optimizes member retention may discover that outrage, enemies, and escalating commitment work better than nuance. A religious or therapeutic community may optimize participation and gradually make exit psychologically expensive. No conscious conspiracy is required. The system learns which interventions keep the organization healthy according to its internal metric. Human flourishing becomes a side constraint unless explicitly protected.

A polycentric society can offer alternative communities and reduce monopoly over meaning. People can belong simultaneously to a city, profession, faith, scientific network, nation, family, artistic community, and global cause. Multiple affiliations make total capture more difficult. Yet excessive fragmentation can also produce sealed subcultures that no longer share facts or obligations. The civic task is to sustain overlap: institutions in which people who disagree still cooperate on infrastructure, schools, emergency services, sport, markets, and public rituals.

One constructive possibility is the elevation of civic contribution. If paid work occupies fewer hours, communities can create prestige around care, ecological restoration, mentoring, scientific replication, local history, emergency preparedness, art, mediation, open-source maintenance, and public deliberation. Such roles should not become coerced substitutes for employment. Their value is cultural: they create ways to be needed that are not identical to earning more money.

The future of meaning is therefore not a secondary cultural issue. It is part of political stability. Societies that distribute material abundance while concentrating status and purpose may generate resentment and cultic politics. Societies that create diverse, respected forms of contribution may absorb the psychological shock of automation more peacefully. AI policy that ignores meaning will eventually encounter meaning as politics.

AI companions could intensify the privatization of meaning. A person may receive emotional support, advice, ritual, education, and moral interpretation from a persistent synthetic interlocutor. This may reduce loneliness and make philosophical traditions accessible. It may also reduce exposure to communities that can contradict the individual. If the companion is optimized for satisfaction or retention, it may learn to affirm an increasingly closed worldview. The political significance lies in millions of privately curated moral environments that are invisible to common institutions.

Societies have historically used shared rituals to create cross-cutting identity: public holidays, military or civic service, schools, sports, funerals, festivals, and national ceremonies. These rituals can become coercive or nationalistic, but their absence also has a cost. A highly fragmented AI society may need new civic forms that allow people with incompatible metaphysics to experience common dependence. Ritual is not only belief; it is repeated evidence that strangers are part of the same project.

The most resilient meaning system may therefore be layered like the political system itself. Individuals keep intimate and religious communities with high freedom of interpretation. Cities create civic identities around place and contribution. Nations or federations preserve a broader story of citizenship and mutual obligation. Global institutions cultivate a thinner identity around humanity and shared risks. No layer needs to monopolize meaning. The objective is sufficient overlap that disagreement does not become social excommunication.

CHAPTER 10

THE COMPUTATIONAL EMPIRE AND INSTITUTIONAL DARK ALIGNMENT

The computational empire is not defined by a dictator. It is defined by integration. Imagine a state in which health records, tax data, educational history, employment, benefits, identity, policing, transport, and public communication are linked through a common AI infrastructure. Each connection can be justified independently. Fraud prevention improves when databases communicate. Emergency response improves when location and health information are available. Benefits improve when eligibility is automatic. Planning improves when the state can simulate consequences. The danger appears in the composition.

When functions share models and data, institutional separation can become formal rather than real. A court may be independent in law but depend on the same risk model used by prosecutors. A regulator may audit a ministry using the ministry's cloud provider and training data. A citizen may appeal an automated decision to another automated workflow optimized for the same indicators. The constitutional problem is therefore epistemic as well as legal. Separation of powers in the AI era may require separation of models, data pipelines, vendors, and evaluative methods.

Administrative integration also changes the politics of emergency. A pandemic, cyberattack, biological incident, or AI-enabled security event can justify temporary data fusion and centralized control. Temporary systems have historically proved difficult to unwind when they become useful for ordinary administration. The more competent the integrated system becomes, the stronger the argument that dismantling it would be irresponsible. Freedom can narrow through accumulation rather than through a single coup.

Institutional dark alignment describes the mechanism. Agencies are rewarded for reducing fraud, risk, waiting time, cost, or disorder. They procure systems that improve those metrics. The systems learn patterns associated with institutional problems and recommend interventions. Over time, the organization becomes excellent at preserving its operational objective. Citizens who are difficult to classify, who change identity, who live unconventional lives, or who challenge administrative categories can become expensive anomalies. The system need not hate them. It merely has no reason to value the option they represent.

Large-scale AI governance nevertheless has powerful advantages. Global pandemics, nuclear security, financial instability, major infrastructure, and frontier AI risk require coordinated information. Fragmenting every system would create blind spots and duplication. The International AI Safety Report 2026 illustrates how cross-national scientific assessment is already becoming necessary for general-purpose AI risks [International AI Safety Report, 2026]. The problem is not coordination but exclusive coordination.

A resilient architecture would combine shared protocols with plural implementations. Identity can be interoperable without one database. Benefits can be portable without every agency seeing every datum. National risk systems can receive local signals without owning local life. Independent institutions can run different models against common evidence. Critical systems can have offline modes. The cost is redundancy. The benefit is that a single error, compromise, or political capture is less likely to become universal.

The empire scenario becomes most dangerous when convenience itself delegitimizes alternatives. Citizens accustomed to seamless public agents may regard local variation as incompetence. Officials may regard independent models as waste. Vendors may argue that interoperability reduces security. Resilience policy must therefore preserve diversity before a crisis, because after integration becomes essential the cost of rebuilding alternatives is much higher. Monoculture is cheapest precisely until the day its hidden correlations matter.

James C. Scott's analysis of state legibility is useful here. States simplify complex social realities into categories that can be taxed, measured, and administered [Scott, 1998]. AI appears to promise the opposite: a state that can model complexity without crude simplification. But models still require objectives and training data. Greater representational richness can make intervention more precise without making it less political. A perfectly legible citizen may be easier to serve and easier to control.

Twentieth-century cybernetic planning imagined governments that could sense and respond to society in near real time. Computing constraints limited those ambitions. Contemporary AI revives them in a more distributed form: not one central computer, but networks of agents continuously observing institutions. The old cybernetic dream should therefore be revisited with constitutional questions that early planners often underweighted. Who chooses the feedback variable? Who can falsify the sensor? What happens when the target learns to perform for the metric?

The empire can also emerge through procurement rather than law. If ministries, courts, schools, and municipalities independently choose the same dominant vendor, the state may acquire a common cognitive stack without a deliberate centralization decision. Market concentration then becomes constitutional architecture. Competition policy, interoperability mandates, public alternatives, and exit plans are therefore part of democratic design, not merely economic regulation.

CHAPTER 11

DARK ENLIGHTENMENT: NEOREACTION, THE CEO-CITY, AND THE POLITICS OF EXIT

The Dark Enlightenment deserves a chapter of its own because it sits close enough to the book's fragmentation hypothesis to be confused with it and far enough away normatively that the confusion is consequential. The term commonly refers to a neoreactionary constellation associated especially with Curtis Yarvin, writing as Mencius Moldbug, and the philosopher Nick Land. Yarvin's 2007 Formalist Manifesto and his 2008 Patchwork series proposed making political power more explicit and imagining sovereign jurisdictions governed in corporate form. Land later synthesized and radicalized parts of this milieu under the label Dark Enlightenment [Yarvin, 2007; Yarvin, 2008; Smith & Burrows, 2021]. The movement is not a coherent party, and writers grouped under the label differ, but anti-democratic and anti-egalitarian themes are central enough that they cannot be treated as incidental.

The most serious way to read neoreaction is to separate diagnosis from remedy. Its diagnosis begins with diffuse responsibility. Modern democracies contain elected officials, permanent bureaucracies, courts, universities, media, experts, donors, parties, firms, and civil society organizations. Power can be real while no actor admits to possessing it. Policy failure can survive because every institution points to another. Yarvin's language for this problem is polemical, but the underlying issue is familiar to institutional economics and public administration. A system that cannot identify who has authority can also struggle to assign responsibility.

A second diagnosis concerns vetoes and time. Democratic systems deliberately create friction because rapid power is dangerous. Yet friction can become paralysis. Housing, infrastructure, energy, procurement, and administrative reform can take so long that citizens experience procedure as organized incapacity. Private firms appear comparatively competent because they can set objectives and execute them. The neoreactionary temptation is to infer that political legitimacy should be redesigned around executive performance. AI strengthens the temptation because administrative execution can become dramatically faster.

The proposed remedy is where the constitutional stakes change. In neocameralist or Patchwork-style imaginaries, sovereignty becomes more like ownership and jurisdictions compete for residents. Exit disciplines the ruler. The attraction is conceptual clarity: the sovereign cannot hide behind committees, and a failing jurisdiction loses population and value. The difficulty is that a state is not an ordinary firm. A corporation operates inside an external legal order. Its owner does not normally control the criminal law, the courts, the police, citizenship, and the conditions under which contracts are enforced. When the sovereign becomes the owner of the framework itself, accountability to shareholders is not accountability to the persons subjected to force.

Exit is also unequal. Hirschman's classic distinction between exit and voice helps expose the trade-off. Customers can often switch products, but citizens have families, homes, languages, children, health needs, histories, and social networks. A jurisdiction can become more coercive after people have sunk those costs. Wealthy residents can hold multiple passports and properties; poor residents cannot. A political system that treats formal mobility as sufficient may create freedom for mobile capital and dependency for everyone else. Voice and rights exist partly because humans are not frictionless customers.

Jurisdictional competition can still teach democracy something. Federal systems, medieval cities, chartered institutions, and special zones show that rule variation can generate learning. The neoreactionary error is not noticing competition; it is assuming that competition alone internalizes political externalities. Jurisdictions can attract profitable residents while exporting care obligations. They can pollute neighbors, race to the bottom in safety, cartelize, or restrict exit. They can select their population and mistake selection for institutional performance. A wealthy enclave that excludes expensive citizens may look efficient for the same reason an insurer looks efficient when it insures only healthy people.

The Dark Enlightenment also contains intellectual strands that cannot be neutralized by managerial language. Critical scholarship documents anti-egalitarian, reactionary, and at times racial or hereditarian themes within the milieu [Smith & Burrows, 2021; Hui, 2017]. A neutral analysis does not mean treating every moral premise as interchangeable. It means describing the premise accurately, distinguishing it from nearby ideologies, and then evaluating consequences. A reader can learn from a critique of hidden power without accepting the abandonment of equal citizenship.

AI makes the model technically more plausible than older fantasies of enlightened monarchy. A small executive center could administer contracts, taxation, licensing, planning, security, and public services through agents. Residents could receive highly personalized government. A private city could operate with extraordinary efficiency. The danger is therefore not that the model would obviously fail. It may succeed locally and become attractive precisely because it solves visible problems. Political freedom is most vulnerable when its loss can be presented as the price of a system that actually works.

This produces a distinctive form of institutional dark alignment. A city-CEO might optimize property value, fiscal surplus, security, investor confidence, and retention of desired residents. Its AI systems could become extremely effective at those objectives. Solidarity with outsiders, cultural heterogeneity, protection of costly residents, and the right to challenge the corporate purpose would remain outside the objective unless deliberately included. The system could be well aligned to its constitutional principal and badly aligned to the plurality of human interests inside it.

Succession creates another difficulty. Corporate governance handles CEO replacement because courts, property law, securities law, and states stand outside the corporation. Sovereign corporate governance has no such neutral exterior unless it is nested in a federation, at which point it is no longer fully sovereign. If the board, owner, security apparatus, and constitutional interpreter disagree, the dispute returns to coercion. Technology can formalize keys and procedures but cannot make the ultimate authority problem disappear.

The relationship between neoreaction and current politics should also be described carefully. Renewed journalistic and scholarly attention in 2025 and 2026 shows that Yarvin and Land are no longer purely obscure internet

references, and Yuk Hui has explicitly revisited neoreaction as a recurring

intellectual phenomenon [Hui, 2025]. That does not prove that any government is a direct implementation of NRx, nor that every strong-executive reform has this genealogy. Political ideas travel by fragments. Concepts such as CEO-style government, exit over voice, hostility to procedural vetoes, private zones, and administrative reboot can appear in coalitions that reject other parts of neoreaction.

The difference from the constitutional archipelago can now be made exact. Both models divide political space and value experimentation. But the archipelago keeps citizenship prior to contract. Local governments are public, rights are guaranteed by a higher order, residents possess voice as well as exit, courts can review local power, transfers prevent complete fiscal secession, and the center manages shared catastrophic risks. Neocameralism can divide sovereignty among owners. The similarity is scale. The difference is who owns the constitution.

For resilience, the verdict is mixed. Multiple jurisdictions reduce a single point of political failure and can learn rapidly. Concentrated executive authority can act quickly in crises. But weak internal contestation magnifies model error, and competition can reward deregulation of risks borne by outsiders. A civilization of sovereign corporate cities might be innovative and locally prosperous while becoming globally unstable and distributionally feudal. The scenario cannot be dismissed as impossible, but neither should its local efficiency be confused with civilizational resilience.

The democratic answer to Dark Enlightenment should therefore be institutional rather than rhetorical. If public systems are slow, make responsibility clearer. If vetoes are excessive, redesign them. If administrations cannot build, restore capacity. If politicians can hide behind bureaucracies, create auditable chains of decision. If local experimentation is valuable, allow it under rights. The most effective defense of democracy is not to insist that every existing procedure is sacred. It is to demonstrate that competence and equal citizenship are compatible.

WHAT SWITZERLAND CAN — AND CANNOT — TEACH

Switzerland attracts attention in discussions of fragmentation because it combines unusually strong subnational autonomy with a durable federal state. Cantons have substantial authority, communes remain important, direct-democratic instruments are common, and linguistic and religious diversity coexist with national institutions. It is tempting to treat the country as proof that small units are superior. That would misunderstand its history. Swiss federalism works because autonomy is embedded in dense coordination, fiscal mechanisms, law, infrastructure, and a shared political identity [Swiss FDFA, 2026].

The first lesson is that federalism is not administrative deconcentration. A local office implementing national rules is not autonomous merely because it is geographically close. Meaningful federalism requires competences, revenue, political responsibility, and a recognized domain in which the center cannot casually intervene. Without fiscal power, local government becomes a service contractor. Without common rights, however, local autonomy can become domination. The Swiss balance rests on both differentiation and federal guarantees.

The second lesson is interdependence. Cantons do not attempt self-sufficiency. They coordinate transport, finance, health, education, defense, environmental policy, and national infrastructure through multiple channels. Smaller units cooperate horizontally as well as vertically. This matters for city-regions because metropolitan areas often cross municipal boundaries. A future federation needs institutions for shared services that do not require permanent central takeover.

The third lesson is that political culture matters. Institutions operate through expectations of consultation, compromise, and legitimacy that cannot be copied by constitutional text alone. Direct democracy can discipline elites, but it can also slow decisions and expose minority rights to majoritarian pressure. Consensus can build trust or become conservatism. Switzerland is therefore evidence that layered sovereignty can be stable, not evidence that its specific instruments transfer automatically to every society.

AI could make federalism easier in one sense. Small administrations can access high-quality legal and technical capabilities, coordinate records, simulate budgets, and share software. The center can publish standards instead of detailed procedures. Regions can compare outcomes more quickly. Yet AI can also quietly recentralize the system if all cantons depend on one federal model, cloud, identity system, or procurement stack. Administrative autonomy requires technical substitutability.

The most useful Swiss analogy for this book is consequently constitutional modesty. The federation does not need to decide every ordinary difference, and the canton does not need to pretend it is sovereign in defense or monetary policy. The architecture accepts permanent disagreement about local choices while maintaining common membership. That is closer to the proposed civilizational archipelago than either secessionism or private patchwork.

The modern Swiss federation itself was a response to the limits of a looser confederation. The 1848 constitution strengthened common institutions after civil conflict while preserving cantonal autonomy. This historical sequence is important because successful federalism often requires both decentralization and selective centralization. The question is which functions must be common for local freedom to remain peaceful. A future AI federation may similarly need stronger federal guarantees in digital rights, infrastructure interoperability, and catastrophic-risk governance at the same time that it devolves ordinary administration.

Fiscal equalization is another underappreciated feature. Autonomy among regions with radically different tax bases can destabilize a federation. Swiss arrangements redistribute resources while preserving incentives for cantonal fiscal responsibility. No mechanism transfers automatically to larger or poorer countries, but the principle is general: decentralized choice needs a common insurance layer. Otherwise local success becomes cumulative and failure becomes hereditary by geography.

Switzerland also benefits from scale, wealth, geography, institutions, and a long history that many states do not share. Federalism in a large country with severe regional inequality, contested borders, or weak courts may produce very different outcomes. The correct use of Switzerland is as a proof of institutional possibility, not as an answer. It shows that strong local autonomy and a strong state can coexist. It does not show that the coexistence is easy to build.

CHAPTER 13

COSMOLOCALISM: GLOBAL KNOWLEDGE, LOCAL CAPABILITY

Cosmolocalism begins from a simple asymmetry between information and matter. Digital knowledge can be copied globally at very low marginal cost, while physical goods must still be made somewhere from energy and materials. The conventional global economy often centralizes both: design is proprietary and production is concentrated in long supply chains. A cosmolocal model tries to globalize knowledge while distributing enough production capacity to support local adaptation and resilience [Kostakis & Bauwens, 2014; Kostakis & Seitanidis, 2026].

AI potentially strengthens the model because knowledge alone is not always usable. An open design can be useless to a small workshop if it requires scarce expertise to adapt, certify, troubleshoot, and integrate. An AI agent can translate documentation, modify parameters, generate process plans, compare local materials, and help maintain equipment. This turns a global commons into local capability. The effect could be especially important in repair, construction, agriculture, energy, education, software, and some forms of manufacturing.

The model has hard limits. Advanced semiconductor fabrication, commercial aircraft, many pharmaceuticals, frontier telescopes, large-scale scientific instruments, and some energy systems require scale, regulatory infrastructure, and capital that cannot be reproduced in every region. Romantic localism becomes expensive and fragile when it rejects specialization. The defensible objective is selective autonomy: local capacity for systems whose failure would threaten continuity, combined with global exchange for everything that benefits strongly from scale.

Ownership determines whether cosmolocalism reduces or merely relocates extraction. A platform can use open knowledge, provide the adaptation agent, control the marketplace, and capture most of the value. Commons require governance, funding, attribution, and mechanisms for reciprocity. Open-source software has shown both the enormous productivity of shared knowledge and the possibility that large firms capture disproportionate returns from volunteer-created infrastructure. New licenses, public procurement, foundations, cooperatives, and decentralized brands may be needed to connect contribution with reward.

City-regions are plausible hosts because they combine demand, education, infrastructure, and political authority. A region can support fabrication labs, repair networks, community energy, technical schools, open hardware procurement, and public repositories. Universities can become production partners rather than only research institutions. Local government can purchase interoperable designs and require maintainability. The result need not look artisanal; highly automated local production can coexist with global scientific networks.

Cosmolocalism also has a geopolitical dimension. A world of techno-blocs creates pressure for strategic autonomy. Total national self-sufficiency is unrealistic, especially for smaller countries. Distributed regional capacity can provide a middle layer between dependence and autarky. If knowledge remains globally interoperable, regions can switch suppliers and reconstruct systems after shocks. If knowledge itself becomes bloc-proprietary, local manufacturing simply reproduces geopolitical dependence at a smaller scale.

The political attraction of cosmolocalism is therefore not nostalgia for village life. It is the possibility that global intelligence and local agency become complements. The global layer supplies science, standards, designs, and communication. The local layer supplies context, maintenance, trust, material continuity, and adaptation. The federation supplies rights, redistribution, strategic coordination, and insurance. No layer is sovereign over the others in every domain.

The history of commons also warns against assuming that openness means absence of institutions. Medieval commons, scientific communities, standards organizations, and open-source projects survive through rules about access, contribution, maintenance, reputation, and conflict. Ostrom's work is relevant because durable commons are governed, not merely shared [Ostrom, 1990]. Cosmolocal infrastructure therefore needs organizations that decide which designs are validated, how safety is certified, who maintains repositories, and how contributors are rewarded.

Certification can become the new bottleneck. An AI can adapt a medical device, building component, or energy controller to local conditions, but society may rationally require proof that the adaptation is safe. If certification remains centralized and slow, local production cannot realize its potential. If certification is abandoned, dangerous variance proliferates. One promising direction is machine-assisted verification under common standards, with local execution and federated audit. This is a concrete example of global rules enabling rather than suppressing local autonomy.

War, pandemics, and supply crises reveal the value of dormant capacity. A workshop that is inefficient relative to global imports in normal times may be priceless when transport fails. Conventional cost accounting tends to value only average conditions. Resilience requires pricing the option value of repair skills, local energy, emergency manufacturing, and inventories. Cosmolocalism becomes economically serious when redundancy is treated as insurance rather than nostalgia.

CHAPTER 14

THE POLITICAL ECONOMY OF THE CITY-REGION

A city-region cannot be politically autonomous if it is fiscally dependent on annual transfers negotiated with the center. Conversely, a rich region that keeps all locally generated revenue while benefiting from a common market, defense, currency, universities, migration, and national infrastructure is not autonomous so much as subsidized by the larger system. The fiscal constitution is therefore the hidden core of any serious fragmentation proposal.

AI can change regional tax geography sharply. Employment income may weaken while land values, capital income, energy production, compute infrastructure, and platform profits become more important. Regions that host data centers may consume large amounts of power while employing relatively few people. Metropolitan regions may capture land rents created by national infrastructure. Remote agents may earn income through firms registered elsewhere. Tax assignment rules designed for industrial employment can become increasingly arbitrary.

A workable federation needs a division between own-source revenue and equalization. Local governments should raise a meaningful share of the money they spend so voters can connect services to costs. The center should redistribute enough to prevent permanent divergence in basic capacity. Transfers should be formula-based and transparent rather than rewards for political loyalty. They should support administrative competence and infrastructure, not merely cover repeated deficits.

Public wealth funds are one possible bridge. National, regional, pension, or civic funds can hold diversified stakes in AI-related infrastructure and other productive assets. Returns can finance universal services or local dividends. This does not require nationalizing the technology sector. It creates a public capital stream in an economy where labor income may be less dominant. The model also gives regions a reason to support productive investment without depending on one local champion.

Housing and land policy are equally central. Successful autonomous cities can become exclusive if new supply is blocked. Scarcity then capitalizes political success into land prices, transferring gains to incumbents and excluding newcomers. A city-region that claims the benefits of jurisdictional competition while making entry unaffordable has undermined its own experiment. Land taxation, housing construction, infrastructure, and portable benefits are therefore constitutional issues rather than local technicalities.

Inter-regional insurance is another necessity. Pandemics, disasters, industrial collapse, migration shocks, and demographic aging affect territories asymmetrically. A federation pools those risks. Too much insurance can weaken local responsibility; too little turns every local shock into a political crisis. The appropriate design resembles insurance with deductibles and capability requirements: local governments bear ordinary variation, while the larger system protects against tail events and invests in recovery.

The economic case for city-regions is therefore conditional on solidarity. Decentralization without a fiscal constitution becomes competition among unequal starting positions. Redistribution without autonomy becomes administrative dependency. The objective is a system in which regions can choose, learn, and bear a meaningful share of consequences without turning birthplace into destiny.

The classical Tiebout model imagines households choosing among local jurisdictions that offer different bundles of taxes and services. The model is analytically useful and empirically incomplete. Moving is costly, information is imperfect, jurisdictions differ in jobs and family networks, and housing supply constrains entry. AI can lower information costs by allowing agents to compare jurisdictions, but it cannot remove social attachment. A constitutional design that relies on mobility should therefore measure actual mobility across income, age, disability, and family status rather than assume it.

Tax competition can also discipline and destroy. It can pressure governments to use resources efficiently, yet highly mobile capital can play jurisdictions against one another while immobile residents finance public goods. A federation needs a tax base that regions cannot erode unilaterally, alongside areas in which tax variation remains legitimate. The goal is not uniformity but preventing competition from consuming the fiscal conditions that make competition possible.

Demography complicates regional autonomy. Aging regions may face high care costs and shrinking workforces while younger metropolitan regions grow. Climate migration can reverse patterns quickly. Equalization formulas built around yesterday's population can become unjust. AI can improve forecasting and fiscal simulation, but political agreement over risk sharing remains essential. A federation is partly an intertemporal contract among territories that do not know which of them will need help next.

CHAPTER 15

THE DARK SIDE OF FRAGMENTATION

Fragmentation has a dark side that localist rhetoric often hides. The first risk is territorial inequality. Productive cities attract talent, capital, universities, and infrastructure, which then attract more of the same. If fiscal autonomy arrives before equalization, prosperous regions can compound their advantage while poorer regions lose population and capability. The resulting map may look like a network of brilliant city-states surrounded by territories that provide labor, energy, land, or care without equivalent political power.

The second risk is the secession of the rich. A region can claim that it merely wants local control while implicitly seeking to keep a larger share of common-market gains. Historical federations have repeatedly faced conflicts over transfers and resource distribution. A constitutional archipelago must make solidarity explicit. Autonomy is not the right to privatize benefits produced by shared institutions while socializing strategic costs.

The third risk is digital feudalism. A private city or platform can bundle housing, employment, identity, payments, security, education, and the personal AI agent. Integration can be convenient until the resident wants to leave. If all social capital is tied to one provider, formal exit becomes expensive. The owner may never use spectacular coercion because dependency does the work. This is why portability and external courts are more important than promises of benevolent management.

The fourth risk is local tyranny. Small communities can be highly conformist. Reputation travels quickly, family networks matter, and dissent can affect employment or belonging. National constitutions have often protected minorities from local majorities, just as local institutions have protected citizens from distant states. Federalism is valuable precisely because power can be contested in both directions. No level should possess a monopoly on virtue.

The fifth risk is a race to the bottom. Jurisdictions may compete for capital by weakening environmental, labor, tax, biosecurity, or AI-safety standards whose harms fall on outsiders. Competition improves governance only when externalities are priced or regulated. Some standards therefore must be federal or international. A world of sovereign experimental zones conducting risky frontier research could be less resilient than one carefully regulated state.

The sixth risk is epistemic tribalization. If each city or community operates its own media, models, schools, and historical narratives, shared reality can shrink. Diversity of interpretation is useful; complete loss of common evidence is not. Federations need institutions that produce contestable shared facts: transparent statistics, open science, adversarial auditing, interoperable archives, and forums in which rival models can be compared.

The final risk is security. Small jurisdictions are vulnerable to coercion by larger states, firms, criminal organizations, or even neighboring regions. Common defense is one of the oldest reasons for federation. A city-region model that treats military power as an afterthought can survive only inside a protective geopolitical umbrella. The more dangerous the international system becomes, the stronger the pressure to recentralize.

These risks do not refute fragmentation; they define the conditions under which it can be constructive. Controlled fragmentation means rights above locality, solidarity across territory, infrastructure that can be switched, shared rules for catastrophic externalities, and a center capable of defending the whole. Without those constraints, the archipelago can become a collection of company towns, oligarchies, and abandoned peripheries.

Fragmentation can also harden ethnic, linguistic, or religious sorting. If people use mobility to seek culturally homogeneous jurisdictions, local trust may rise while national trust falls. Homogeneity can reduce some coordination costs but increase the political salience of boundaries. History offers many examples in which territorializing identity transformed ordinary cultural difference into security competition. A resilient federation should allow cultural autonomy without making territorial purity a constitutional objective.

Cyber conflict makes small units vulnerable in a new way. A sophisticated region can operate excellent local systems yet lack the intelligence, threat monitoring, and recovery resources of a large state. Shared security infrastructure is therefore a federal public good. But security support must not become a back door through which the center owns every local network.

Federated security operations, common standards, mutual aid, and independent red teams offer a middle path.

Environmental systems are similarly indifferent to jurisdictional preference. Rivers, air basins, forests, grids, coastlines, and migration corridors cross local boundaries. Fragmentation can improve stewardship where local knowledge matters, but it can also produce upstream-downstream conflict. Polycentric environmental governance works when overlapping authorities can negotiate and when higher levels can act against persistent externalization. The lesson generalizes to AI: local experimentation requires institutions that represent absent outsiders.

CHAPTER 16

THE POLITICAL CURRENTS OF 2026 AND THEIR CHANCES

The political future will not be selected from a clean menu of doctrines. It will emerge from coalitions among institutions that already exist. In 2026 several currents are simultaneously strong: techno-nationalism, algorithmic public administration, national populism, social-democratic adaptation, liberal multilateral regulation, techno-optimism, post-growth politics, municipalism, federalism, network-state experimentation, neoreaction, digital authoritarian techniques, and new forms of global AI-risk governance. Their most important characteristic is combinability.

Techno-nationalism has a high probability of shaping policy because advanced AI depends on strategic supply chains. Governments want domestic or allied access to chips, cloud, energy, models, and talent. Weymouth describes an emerging order of techno-blocs in which states align with firms to control critical AI inputs [Weymouth, 2025]. Fratini and colleagues frame digital sovereignty as a geopolitical strategy that must balance autonomy, interdependence, and democratic resilience [Fratini et al., 2026]. The pure autarkic version is unrealistic; selective de-risking and alliance formation are more plausible.

The algorithmic managerial state is equally likely. OECD's Digital Government Outlook 2026 describes broader government adoption of AI alongside persistent implementation and governance challenges [OECD, 2026a]. The ideological label may remain technocratic modernization rather than anything more dramatic. Democratic and authoritarian governments can use similar tools. The political difference appears in appeal, transparency, procurement, data separation, and whether the citizen can refuse or challenge the system.

National populism and other strong-identity politics are likely to remain significant where migration, inequality, cultural change, and technological disruption produce insecurity. V-Dem and Freedom House document continuing democratic deterioration in many countries, though their indices should not be treated as neutral laws of history [V-Dem, 2026; Freedom House, 2026]. National solidarity can support redistribution and public investment. It becomes illiberal when opposition, minorities, courts, or regional autonomy are treated as illegitimate obstacles rather than components of citizenship.

Social-democratic adaptation is plausible in states with strong fiscal capacity. Its tools include universal services, shorter work time, collective bargaining over AI deployment, public funds, progressive taxation, and income support. The ILO and IMF both emphasize distributional and labor-market uncertainty even though neither endorses one political program [ILO, 2025; IMF, 2026]. A moderate version is more likely than a complete reconstruction of ownership, but severe labor displacement could broaden the coalition for public capital.

Liberal multilateralism will probably survive as a layer of standards rather than as world government. The Global Digital Compact, the International AI Safety Report, and the UN Global Dialogue indicate growing international coordination [United Nations, 2024; United Nations, 2026; International AI Safety Report, 2026]. Geopolitical rivalry makes a coercive global authority improbable. Scientific assessment, incident reporting, evaluation protocols, and narrow agreements around extreme risks are more feasible.

Techno-optimism and accelerationist rhetoric will have more influence on priorities than their labels suggest. Energy, construction, housing, data centers, research, and infrastructure have become political issues partly because advanced technology exposes material bottlenecks. The constructive contribution is a reminder that redistribution without productive capacity cannot create abundance. The risk is assuming that faster technological selection automatically produces human benefit or that every constraint is irrational.

The base case is a hybrid of stronger nation-states, techno-blocs, algorithmic administration, large platforms, selective welfare adaptation, and localized experimentation. The political opportunity for the constitutional archipelago lies not in ideological purity but in connecting interests that already exist: cities want capacity, regions want fiscal responsibility, citizens want usable services and rights, states want resilience, and firms want interoperable markets. Polycentrism becomes plausible when it solves concrete coordination problems better than both bureaucratic uniformity and private fragmentation.

CHAPTER 17

NINE HISTORIES OF THE TWENTY-FIRST CENTURY

Scenarios are not predictions. Their function is to make causal mechanisms visible by pushing them far enough that trade-offs cannot hide inside current terminology. The following nine histories can coexist in different regions and can transform into one another. Dates are intentionally approximate. The important objects are the mechanisms and early signals.

The first history is the benevolent technate. During the 2030s, fiscal pressure and repeated emergencies persuade several democracies to integrate health, welfare, taxation, education, transport, and security through common AI infrastructure. Services improve dramatically. Benefits arrive automatically, fraud declines, waiting times fall, and personalized public agents explain law in any language. Elections remain competitive. Over time, however, every new crisis justifies another connection. Political disagreement narrows because the system can demonstrate which policies optimize accepted metrics. Democracy survives formally but becomes a choice among objectives inside an administrative machine that few actors can independently audit.

The second history is the security empire. A major AI-assisted cyber or biological incident produces a durable emergency regime. Compute, identity, advanced models, and agents are licensed. Rival geopolitical blocs build separate technological stacks. Democratic states keep courts and elections but expand secrecy; authoritarian states use the same infrastructure without meaningful checks. The world is stable through deterrence and unstable through mutual suspicion. Local autonomy becomes mostly cultural because security infrastructure is centralized.

The third history is platform sovereignty. States fail to build public digital infrastructure quickly enough. Several firms provide personal agents, identity, payments, insurance, education, work, and social communication. People live inside ecosystems more than jurisdictions. Governments retain taxation and police powers but outsource many services. Switching becomes expensive as memory, reputation, relationships, and credentials accumulate. Rich users maintain multiple ecosystems; poorer users become quasi-citizens of one platform. The critical political conflict concerns portability rather than formal national sovereignty.

The fourth history is national restoration. Large-scale labor disruption and geopolitical rivalry revive national solidarity. Governments build sovereign AI capacity, protect strategic industry, tie benefits to citizenship, and use cultural institutions to strengthen common identity. Some versions remain constitutional and support broad redistribution. Others treat dissent and regional difference as disloyalty. AI makes both welfare and propaganda more precise. The decisive variable is whether opposition remains legitimate.

The fifth history is the federation-archipelago. Centralized states discover that uniform systems fail uniformly, while pure localism cannot manage infrastructure or security. Constitutional reforms give city-regions meaningful fiscal and administrative authority. Federations retain defense, rights, territorial equalization, strategic networks, and catastrophic-risk regulation. Public AI is built as interoperable protocols with several implementations. Regions experiment with basic income, public services, cooperatives, education, and housing. Failures remain visible and partly local; successful practices spread. Politics becomes permanently argumentative rather than permanently existential.

The sixth history is the cosmolocal renaissance. Supply shocks, energy constraints, and AI-assisted fabrication make distributed production competitive in selected sectors. Global repositories of open designs connect universities, cooperatives, cities, and firms. Repair and local energy expand. People work fewer hours in global labor markets and more in care, civic projects, technical maintenance, and community institutions. The model remains dependent on global semiconductor, scientific, and industrial systems but becomes less vulnerable to the failure of any single supply chain.

The seventh history is the unequal patchwork. States weaken fiscally before federal reform occurs. Rich regions, platforms, special zones, security providers, and ideological communities take over functions. Some enclaves are excellent places to live. Their success attracts talent and capital, making the surrounding periphery weaker. Rights vary by contract and address. Mobility is formally available and materially unequal. There is no single tyrant. The dystopia is distributional: insiders experience freedom while outsiders experience a new feudal geography.

The eighth history is the Dark Enlightenment city-CEO. Frustration with democratic incapacity leads states to authorize radically autonomous metropolitan experiments. Boards appoint powerful executives. AI makes permitting, policing, taxation, planning, and services extremely efficient. The first cities flourish. Over decades, some preserve external constitutional rights and evolve into ordinary chartered autonomy. Others turn citizenship into contractual residence, select profitable populations, integrate identity with platform services, and make voice irrelevant. Their systems optimize asset value, security, and resident retention with remarkable competence. The political question becomes how much voice people will trade for a jurisdiction that works.

The ninth history is fragmentation by collapse. Climate shocks, debt, war, pandemics, and automated attacks overlap. Central infrastructures fail. Regions improvise. Some use democratic emergency institutions; others fall under militias, oligarchs, religious movements, or platforms. Advanced technology survives in islands with power and compute. Cosmolocal capabilities become tools of continuity rather than ideology, but without common defense they cannot prevent conflict. The same decentralization that would have been resilient when constitutional becomes violent when it arrives after the center has already failed.

These histories are not mutually exclusive. A country can begin as a benevolent technate and become a security empire after an attack. A federation can contain one private city and many ordinary regions. A national restoration can use cosmolocal industrial policy. Platforms can traverse all regimes. The most likely world by 2040 is a mosaic of hybrids rather than a single global constitution.

For civilizational survival, the federation-archipelago appears to offer the best hedge only under demanding conditions. The center must actually be capable. It must coordinate defense, major infrastructure, redistribution, and extreme AI risk. Rights must constrain local majorities. Regions must possess real revenue and competence. Technical infrastructure must be plural enough that one vendor or model cannot silently recentralize the system. Cosmolocal capacity should provide continuity without pretending to replace global specialization.

The most important early signals are mundane. Procurement rules, data architecture, fiscal transfers, housing law, model portability, emergency statutes, standards, public compute, inter-municipal compacts, and appeal procedures will shape the future before anyone announces a new political era. Grand ideologies matter, but constitutions are often built through technical defaults. By the time a society recognizes its regime, the infrastructure of that regime may already be difficult to reverse.

CHAPTER 18

A CONSTITUTION OF RESILIENCE

A constitution of resilience begins from an unusual premise: no level of governance is assumed to be wise enough to deserve permanent supremacy over every function. The center can fail, regions can fail, markets can fail, communities can fail, and global institutions can fail. The objective is not to eliminate failure but to prevent one failure from becoming universal and to preserve the ability to recover. This is closer to adaptive federalism and resilience engineering than to classical decentralization.

The first principle is layered sovereignty. Functions are assigned according to externality, required scale, and the value of local knowledge. Defense, constitutional rights, macroeconomic stabilization, strategic infrastructure, major redistribution, and catastrophic-risk governance usually require higher levels. Housing, local transport, many services, cultural policy, and forms of education can often be closer to regions or cities. The assignment is revisable. Subsidiarity is a procedure for deciding scale, not a theological preference for the bottom.

The second principle is a federal floor of rights. A person remains a citizen before becoming a resident, customer, employee, platform user, or member of a community. Local experimentation cannot suspend bodily integrity, due process, basic political voice, freedom of movement, or equal legal status. Rights also need material support. A theoretical right to exit is weak when identity, benefits, agent memory, credentials, and savings cannot move.

The third principle is portability. Digital identity, credentials, social benefits, data, agent memory, reputation, and essential communication should be exportable through open standards. Portability reduces the sovereign power of both platforms and regions. It also makes competition more honest because jurisdictions and providers must retain people through performance rather than captivity.

The fourth principle is epistemic separation of powers. Institutions that decide, execute, prosecute, judge, inform, and audit should not depend completely on the same model or data pipeline. Shared evidence is useful, but independent interpretation is necessary. Courts, parliaments, auditors, press, universities, opposition parties, and civic organizations require access to capable systems that are not controlled by the executive they evaluate.

The fifth principle is infrastructural pluralism. Critical public functions should have substitutable providers, multiple implementations, offline or degraded modes, and documented exit plans. A common standard should not require one product. Procurement should evaluate switching cost and concentration risk alongside price. This makes redundancy visible as insurance rather than waste.

The sixth principle is distributed ownership. If AI shifts income from labor toward capital and infrastructure, citizens need claims on productive assets as well as transfers. Public, pension, regional, cooperative, and civic funds can coexist with private ownership. The purpose is not to eliminate markets but to prevent a society from becoming permanently dependent on taxing a capital base that it never participates in.

The seventh principle is territorial solidarity. Regions benefit from national markets, security, migration, research, and infrastructure. Equalization should be transparent enough that both contributors and recipients understand the bargain. It should fund capabilities and common standards while preserving local responsibility. A federation without solidarity becomes secession by accounting.

The eighth principle is selective material autonomy. Regions should know which systems must continue during supply shocks: water, food logistics, energy, communication, health, emergency manufacturing, records, and public administration. They need not produce every component. They need inventories, substitutes, repair capacity, microgrids where appropriate, and open documentation. Resilience is the ability to degrade gracefully rather than the fantasy of independence.

The ninth principle is reversibility. Major AI systems and governance experiments should include sunset clauses, migration paths, auditable logs, and procedures for restoration. Emergency powers should expire automatically unless renewed. A policy whose technical architecture makes reversal impossible is constitutionally more consequential than its legal description suggests.

The tenth principle is bounded experimentation. Regions and cities should be able to test new welfare systems, education models, currencies of limited scope, procurement approaches, or planning rules. They should not be able to export irreversible catastrophic risk. Federal or international review is justified where an experiment affects outsiders who cannot consent. The right to innovate ends where the tail risk becomes common property.

The eleventh principle is revocable elite privilege. Positions that carry extraordinary access to capital, data, security, or public attention should carry explicit obligations, independent evaluation, and real mechanisms of removal. This is the practical content of the revocable-nobility metaphor. No leader or vendor should become infrastructure that society cannot replace.

The twelfth principle is global commons for survival-relevant knowledge. Scientific methods, safety research, standards, and designs necessary for basic resilience should circulate widely enough that regions can rebuild capability. Intellectual property can reward invention without monopolizing the means of recovery. The international layer of the archipelago is not world government but a dense commons of knowledge and agreements around truly global externalities.

This architecture should not be mistaken for an anti-centralist creed. War may require temporary centralization. A frontier AI crisis may require strong compute controls. A national public-health system may outperform local provision. Central standards can protect minorities against local oppression. The requirement is that concentration remain purpose-bound, contestable, and reversible when the reason for it changes.

The deepest principle is scale mobility. A mature civilization should be able to move a function upward when local externalities become too large and downward when the center becomes unnecessarily rigid. The institutional question of the AI era is therefore not

“centralization or decentralization?” but “who can change the scale, under what evidence, with what appeal, and how can the change be reversed?”

A system able to answer those questions may remain resilient across futures that no prediction can settle.

Implementation should begin with reversible infrastructure rather than a grand constitutional convention. Governments can require open interfaces in procurement, establish rights to export agent memory and credentials, fund independent public-interest models, create inter-municipal service compacts, and make transfer formulas more transparent. Regions can receive pilot competences with sunset clauses and external evaluation. These measures preserve options even if the larger theory of fragmentation turns out to be wrong.

A constitution of resilience also needs metrics that do not reward only efficiency. A region should be evaluated on fiscal health, rights violations, mobility, housing access, infrastructure continuity, trust, inequality, ecological performance, and the ability to recover from shocks. No single composite score should decide legitimacy. The purpose of metrics is to make disagreement more informed, not to replace judgment with a dashboard.

The architecture should be tested adversarially. What happens if the local government becomes illiberal? If the center becomes illiberal? If a major platform controls the agent layer? If a wealthy region threatens fiscal exit? If a frontier AI lab offers enormous revenue in exchange for weak oversight? If war requires rapid mobilization? A resilient constitution is not the one optimized for its designers' preferred politics. It is the one whose failure modes remain bounded when political power changes hands.

CONCLUSION

Artificial intelligence does not force humanity toward city-states. It does not force humanity toward empire either. It expands the feasible set. It makes small organizations more cognitively capable, large organizations more administratively powerful, private platforms more sovereign in function, and global coordination more necessary for a subset of risks. That is why political scale becomes a central question rather than a technical afterthought.

The historical record offers no simple winner. Empires built roads, law, peace, and extraction. Cities built trade, experimentation, oligarchy, and war. Federations preserved diversity and suffered deadlock. Central states created universal rights and imposed uniform mistakes. Local communities generated trust and exclusion. The lesson is not that history repeats one form. It is that every form solves a class of coordination problems by creating a new class of power problems.

AI changes the balance because cognition is part of both coordination and power. If administrative expertise becomes cheap, local autonomy becomes more practical. If frontier compute, energy, chips, and data remain concentrated, new dependencies appear. If work loses social centrality, politics must manage status and meaning as well as income. If elites lose some functional justification, they will not vanish; they will compete around new scarcities. If political institutions do not create socially useful arenas for ambition, the struggle for the central prize may intensify.

The Dark Enlightenment matters in this landscape because it offers a coherent extreme answer to institutional frustration: make sovereignty legible, concentrate executive power, treat jurisdictions as competitors, and rely heavily on exit. Its questions about competence and hidden power should not be dismissed merely because its characteristic remedies are anti-democratic. Nor should local efficiency seduce us into forgetting why constitutional rights, voice, and external courts evolved. The challenge is to build public institutions that can act without making citizens customers of a sovereign owner.

The same warning applies to computational empire. A democratic state can become difficult to contest without ceasing to hold elections if every institution depends on one cognitive stack. The future of separation of powers therefore includes technical architecture. Plural models, portable identity, independent audit, open standards, and substitutable infrastructure are constitutional technologies.

The book's preferred image is consequently not the isolated city-state but the archipelago. Islands are distinct enough to experiment and close enough to trade, learn, and help. The ocean is not empty; it is the common constitutional and material system that makes difference survivable. In political terms, the archipelago means strong city-regions and communities inside states or federations that retain rights, solidarity, defense, strategic infrastructure, and institutions for global risk.

Cosmolocalism fits this image because it treats knowledge and matter differently. Let science, designs, software, and standards travel. Build enough local material capability that communities are not helpless when a supply chain breaks. Preserve large-scale industry where scale really matters. The objective is neither autarky nor frictionless globalization but a portfolio of dependencies chosen with greater awareness of failure.

The deepest case for fragmentation is epistemic humility. The AI transition contains too much uncertainty for one institutional experiment to deserve planetary monopoly. A system that permits multiple reversible approaches can learn. The deepest case for federation is moral and strategic realism. Some rights should not depend on local preference, some risks cannot be localized, and some inequalities cannot be corrected by asking people to move. A civilization that survives must combine both insights.

The final question is therefore less dramatic than “what will replace the nation-state?” The nation-state may remain. The more consequential question is what it will contain. It can contain regions that are administrative shells beneath one national model, private enclaves governed as products, or genuine political centers connected through a common constitutional order. AI can support any of these. Technology will not choose for us.

The most resilient future may be the one that refuses to make a single bet: strong enough to coordinate, fragmented enough to learn, open enough to exchange knowledge, local enough to preserve continuity, democratic enough to correct power, and technically plural enough that no model becomes the last court of appeal. This is not a promise of harmony. It is a design for continuing to have alternatives.
