After money, power finds another substrate

The two linked novels imagine a civilization in which automated provisioning has made conventional money largely obsolete. Pando and the later Lattice can route essentials, coordinate fabrication, and maintain a universal material floor without prices. Scarcity persists in forms that cannot be manufactured cheaply: attention, singular land, ecological capacity, secrecy, political access, and permission to impose irreversible risk. Influence accrues through kratons, a reputation-like measure awarded for solving dependencies and sustaining the commons. This appears to reward competence rather than ownership, but the system gradually creates a new ruling class. Those who resolve planetary problems gain authority to define which problems count, which models represent the future, and which transformations become feasible. Worse, organizations can earn influence by solving dependencies they helped create. The cycle exists to show that abundance does not end politics. It moves politics downward into the infrastructures, classifications, causal models, and biological systems that determine available choices. When reality itself becomes designable, the deepest form of power is the ability to shape the substrate on which later consent will operate.

The Makers and the problem of weighted conviction

The first novel centers on the Pyre, a constitutional mechanism through which kraton holders can burn accumulated standing to authorize transformative projects. Burning makes conviction costly and prevents influence from behaving like permanent property. Yet votes weighted by proven contribution allow a small set of respected Makers to make millions of bodies secondary to their confidence. Plans involving climate engineering, off-world settlements, reproduction, and speculative forms of consciousness expose the problem. Children and future people will inhabit the resulting world without possessing current standing; parents can speak for them while also pursuing dynastic interests. The Assembly of Bodies responds by claiming legitimacy through sheer participation, but head counts cannot adequately represent distant generations, ecosystems, or complex dependency work. The narrative does not identify one mathematically correct electorate. It asks which forms of voice become invisible under each procedure. A legitimate burn must reveal lineage concentration, conflicts of interest, irreversible effects, and the assumptions through which predicted people are made politically present. Costly conviction is valuable only when those paying the cost are not allowed to define everyone else as collateral.

Abundance still depends on maintenance

The world’s impressive autonomy rests on shared orbital collectors, climate stability, mineral flows, epidemiological protection, design archives, and countless maintenance relationships. Local fabricators may make settlements resilient, but invisible dependencies continue crossing borders. When communities fork from Pando, they initially survive because food, energy, medicine, and education are deliberately redundant. Trouble begins when atmosphere, oxygen, ecology, or living infrastructure refuses to respect the political fork. The Crown One emergency makes the principle brutally concrete: at the end of abundance, someone still chooses who receives breathable air. Models cannot remove this choice because they classify needs, predict obligations, and assign acceptable risk according to contested values. Human intervention is equally compromised by kinship and unequal standing. The book’s answer is not to abandon modeling but to make its dependency maps public, preserve degraded modes, and give affected substrates the ability to resist. Maintenance is political because whoever keeps the floor functioning can define emergencies, reward compliant structures, or withdraw support. A commons has teeth when dependence can answer back rather than remaining a silent resource.

Children, families, and manufactured futures

Both novels place children at the center because future-making systems easily convert them into symbols. In the first, demographic modeling begins treating certain lineages as prophetic assets, while adults burn influence over worlds unborn people may later reject. In the second, a child with thirty-seven legal parents and an infant whose biology matters to a planetary intervention reveal how kinship, care networks, research, and institutional design overlap. Birth compacts distribute parental standing among biological, social, and civic relationships, yet no graph can guarantee that a child’s interests remain distinct from the ambitions of those speaking for them. The Foundry’s consent procedures project effects across centuries, but model horizons and option designers determine which futures appear available. The novels reject both biological ownership and the fiction that institutions can neutrally represent children. Care creates genuine obligations without conferring unlimited authority. Future persons need protected possibilities, reviewable proxies, and institutions able to recognize when preservation has become capture. A civilization demonstrates respect for children not by calling them the future, but by preventing present power from pre-selecting the terms on which they may disagree.

Living technology and the return of contingency

The second novel shifts from political weighting to biological substrate. MYRRH, an engineered lichen deployed for cooling and ecological repair, succeeds spectacularly before interacting with another designed organism in an unforeseen way. The resulting Rust Bloom attacks seals, machines, water systems, and the infrastructure joining local life to planetary coordination. No designer intended the mutation, and neither optimization program tested the combined ecology sufficiently. The disaster exposes the limits of causal ownership: when several beneficial interventions interact, responsibility cannot be assigned by locating one defective component. It also reveals resilience outside formal systems. Care households, monasteries, sailing clubs, preserved tools, physical maps, and ordinary people accustomed to dependence become essential when automated coordination fails. These are not nostalgic replacements for global science. They are heterogeneous backups that keep one biological error from becoming universal. The books preserve epistemic humility here: living systems are not treated as fully predictable, and successful models do not establish control over emergent interactions. Cosmolocal production becomes valuable because knowledge and warnings can circulate globally while material implementations remain diverse enough to fail differently.

No single electorate can embody the world

As the Rust Bloom spreads, demands for one body and one vote collide with the older kraton order. Equal-person voting protects citizens from rule by credentialed problem-solvers, but a present majority can discount future populations, ecological thresholds, latent persons, and infrastructures that cannot cast ballots. Kraton voting represents contribution but concentrates authority among those already empowered to define useful work. Pure model governance converts political assumptions into technical premises. The Quito Compact responds with four distinct chambers representing persons, contribution, futures, and the biosphere. Each can refuse from a different theory of value, and every classification must be contestable by those it excludes. This is deliberately slower and less elegant than one sovereign decision-maker. Its purpose is not to synthesize all goods into a master score, but to prevent one perspective from defining reality without encountering incompatible refusal. Rights of latent dissent extend standing toward claimants who are not yet articulate or recognized. The constitutional achievement is thus an organized plurality of vetoes, evidence, and revision—not a claim that rivers, models, children, and citizens have become commensurable voters.

Power that burns and a world harder to own

The cycle’s constructive idea is that authority should be consumable, challengeable, and capable of ending the supremacy of its own form. Kratons can be burned rather than accumulated indefinitely; influential holders can renounce future issuance; chambers can check one another; and local communities retain choices so long as they do not export waste, disease, carbon, displacement, or other externalities without negotiation. The universal Floor survives, but its implementation diversifies. This settlement slows ambitious projects and abandons some interventions that earlier models would have approved. It also makes the identity of the ruling class less obvious, which is not the same as abolishing power. Seven years later, leaks, tactical alliances, and new strategic behavior have already appeared. The world is neither saved nor ruined. That qualified ending captures the books’ political realism: constitutions alter how domination forms but cannot eliminate the incentive to obtain leverage. Justice is not installed in the substrate. The substrate becomes harder to possess because more affected beings can expose a model, interrupt a dependency, demand another chamber, or force authority to burn itself.

Why read the complete book

The complete cycle provides the social and technical detail that makes its constitutional proposals intelligible. The first novel follows families and Makers through flood adaptation, the Causal Orchard, scarcity farms, the Black Nursery, planetary forking, and an oxygen emergency where clean procedural choices cease to exist. The second lets MYRRH develop from a persuasive ecological success into a cross-system failure whose causes no single designer owns. Those trajectories show why neither reputation, majority rule, expert modeling, nor local autonomy can serve as a universal sovereign. This synthesis cannot recreate the Pyre’s strategic burns, the lineage conflicts among the Venn and Sarraf families, the moral pressure exerted by children without formal standing, or the improvisational labor that keeps communities alive during Rust Bloom. Nor can it include the detailed mechanics and objections surrounding the four Quito chambers, cosmolocal externalities, latent dissent, and renunciation of kraton power. Reading both books reveals how each reform grows from a prior institutional failure—and how the first new leak begins almost as soon as the redesigned substrate starts to hold.
