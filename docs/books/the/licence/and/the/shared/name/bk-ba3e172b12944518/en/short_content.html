The missing institution above open code

The manuscript begins from a productive mismatch: software can be copied at negligible marginal cost, but trust accumulates through identifiable responsibility, reliable service, shared history, and remedies when promises fail. Free software and open source distributed important rights to inspect, modify, run, and redistribute code. They did not automatically distribute customers, revenue, infrastructure, operational data, certification, or reputation. Companies can therefore comply with open licences, contribute genuine engineering, and still concentrate value through complementary assets. The book does not describe this outcome as theft, condemn permissive licensing, or deny corporate support for the commons. Its narrower claim is that legal freedom at the code layer does not create distributive fairness at the market layer. Between the proprietary brand and an economically undefended commons, it identifies a possible institutional space: autonomous organisations might share a public name and its promise while accepting reciprocal duties, without becoming subsidiaries or restricting outsiders’ freedom to use the underlying software.

Licences create boundaries, not complete communities

The history of software ownership is used to clarify what different legal instruments actually accomplish. Once software became separable from hardware, copyright enabled controlled distribution; GNU copyleft then used those exclusive rights to preserve defined freedoms downstream. Permissive licences instead maximize the current recipient’s options and can accelerate diffusion, standardization, and commercial integration. Linux demonstrates how GPL can operate as a dependable non-closure pact among strangers and competitors while strong technical leadership, commercial distributions, proprietary applications, and a separately governed mark coexist around it. The book resists mythology: GPL helped protect a common technical core, but did not guarantee democratic governance, equal influence, maintainer funding, customer access, or revenue sharing. Even the right to fork may be economically weak when the departing group loses the recognized name, registry, conferences, distribution channels, and credibility. The larger lesson is functional pluralism: copyright permission, contractual promises, trademark control, governance, and provenance solve different problems and should not be collapsed into one supposedly universal licence.

Openness became an engine of adoption and capture

The commercial reframing of open source showed that permission itself can be a distribution channel. Developers can evaluate and embed a technology before procurement, helping it become a standard while the supplier later sells scarce complements such as reliable operation, integration, compliance, assurance, or indemnity. This strategy has produced valuable public infrastructure, but it can also let the language of community obscure unilateral control of the roadmap, mark, relicensing power, or customer relationship. Cloud computing sharpened the asymmetry by monetizing operation rather than copies, prompting projects and companies to adopt stronger copyleft, source-available terms, or new licensing combinations. The manuscript treats those responses as economically intelligible while noting the institutional tension created when concentrated rightsholders can change future terms after communities contributed under an open identity. “Capture” is therefore defined functionally, not as profit or infringement: it arises when complementary power weakens others’ ability to negotiate, continue, or exit while common maintenance costs remain externalized.

Move reciprocity from code to the official name

The central proposal is the Decentralised Brand Compact, explicitly presented as an unvalidated research architecture rather than an existing licence or ready legal product. Code would remain under recognized open-source licences, available to anyone without membership, revenue payments, or field-of-use restrictions. Additional obligations would attach voluntarily to organisations claiming official participation under the shared mark. Those nodes could be required to meet published standards, support common infrastructure, disclose their relationship to the commons, maintain provenance records, accept audit and remediation, and contribute to a common budget. Independent competitors could still use the software under their own names, and a separate certification mark could distinguish technical compatibility from political and economic membership. The design borrows selectively from franchising, cooperatives, federations, collective and certification marks, standards bodies, foundations, and open-source projects. Its intended novelty is the combination: coherence without a sovereign franchisor, cooperation without one compulsory ownership form, and conditional reputation without enclosure of functionality.

AI turns a licence into a genealogy

Artificial intelligence makes the single-licence fiction especially untenable. Sources, datasets, labels, tokenizers, training code, recipes, checkpoints, weights, adapters, merges, quantizations, outputs, and services may carry different rights, contracts, uncertainties, and person-related obligations. Open weights can enable local inference and adaptation while revealing little about training provenance or reproducibility; compute and operational feedback may remain concentrated. The book therefore proposes describing openness as a profile rather than a badge and recording permissions for concrete operations such as fine-tuning, hosting, distillation, redistribution, or synthetic-data generation. Its rights and provenance manifest would connect each assertion to an artefact, version, claimed legal basis, evidence, transformation history, and confidence level. Crucially, a manifest would document uncertainty rather than manufacture ownership, clear privacy rights, or turn “unknown” into “permitted.” AI agents might help inspect rights graphs, flag incompatible combinations, and administer evidence, but final responsibility, contested judgments, and legal interpretation must remain accountable to people and institutions.

Build an economy and custody that can be contested

The compact’s economic proposal rejects both romantic volunteerism and a universal mathematical measure of merit. Autonomous nodes would retain most customer revenue because they employ staff, sell, deliver, and assume liability, while a defined contribution would fund maintenance, assurance, continuity reserves, and contributor distributions. Current work should receive dependable contracts or grants; historical contribution may receive bounded recognition based on plural evidence, review, decay, materiality thresholds, and appeal. Commits alone cannot value security, documentation, design, governance, data stewardship, or institutional repair. Governance likewise separates powers: a limited mark custodian, node and contributor chambers, independent assurance, transparent budgets, succession arrangements, recoverable infrastructure, conflict rules, and narrow expiring emergency authority. Exit should preserve open-source rights, owned assets, customer relationships subject to law, and portable evidence of past contribution while ending current use of the official mark. Constitutional succession would be exceptional and demanding, not a licence for every minority to duplicate public identity.

Why read the complete book

The complete book provides the historical distinctions, legal cautions, economic reasoning, and implementation detail needed to judge whether the compact could work. Its accounts of GNU, BSD, Linux, Netscape, cloud services, relicensing disputes, foundations, certification regimes, data licensing, open-weight models, and viable forks show why no single moral story explains openness or capture. The full work also examines competition law, trademark quality control, privacy, contributor agreements, taxation, franchise risk, governance failure, funding concentration, customer continuity, and jurisdictional variation—qualifications essential to a proposal that the author repeatedly says requires specialist drafting and empirical testing. Tables, scenarios, pilot terms, budget rules, contribution classes, succession mechanisms, and the machine-readable provenance manifest expose difficult operational questions hidden by the attractive phrase “shared name.” They also clarify the proposed 2026–2035 research agenda and the competing futures—concentrated Empire, legally costly Labyrinth, or interoperable Archipelago—that make experimentation worth considering.
