RAG as an information-flow system

The book exists because Retrieval-Augmented Generation has expanded from a particular model class into a broad family of systems. Two products called RAG may share little beyond supplying external information to a generator. One may preserve source structure, combine lexical and semantic search, rerank candidates, enforce access rules, verify citations, and log every transformation. Another may place five nearby chunks into a prompt. Treating them as equivalent hides the architectural choices that determine accuracy, reproducibility, security, latency, and cost.

RAG is therefore defined as controlled information flow rather than a magical retrieve-then-answer box. Sources are acquired and normalized; documents are parsed and segmented; evidence units enter one or more indexes; queries are interpreted or rewritten; candidate pools are retrieved and reranked; context is selected, ordered, compressed, and labelled; a model generates; verification checks claims, citations, or constraints; and users receive both an answer and some trace of its basis. Each contract can introduce error, so end-to-end quality must be explained through stages rather than attributed vaguely to retrieval or generation.

Retrieval begins before the query

Corpus acquisition sets the ceiling of recall. A system cannot retrieve an article it never indexed, a correction disconnected from its original, or a table destroyed during parsing. Scientific collections may need papers, patents, standards, supplementary files, datasets, code, protocols, reviews, errata, retractions, and version histories. PDF extraction can scramble reading order, detach captions, flatten tables, and corrupt technical identifiers. Robust ingestion keeps original files, normalized representations, structural coordinates, stable identifiers, hashes, and uncertainty about transformation so evidence can be traced to what a user can inspect.

No single retrieval method dominates every query. Sparse methods preserve exact terms and rare identifiers; dense methods recover semantic similarity; hybrid systems exploit their different errors; late-interaction models retain token-level matching; cross-encoders improve expensive final ranking. A practical stack behaves like a recall-preserving funnel: several channels build a broad candidate pool, fusion and reranking estimate local usefulness, and later selection constructs a bounded context. Premature pruning is especially dangerous in multi-hop research because evidence discarded early cannot be recovered by a clever generator. Every narrowing step should remain measurable and attributable.

Context, grounding, and failure attribution

Retrieved passages are only an evidence proposal. Context construction must decide what fits an effective—not merely advertised—token budget, how sources are ordered, how duplicates and distractors are handled, and whether material is grouped by claim, chronology, method, or disagreement. Longer context can reduce performance when relevant evidence is buried or crowded. Grounded generation must distinguish source statements from synthesis, preserve qualifications, support abstention, and attach citations at a granularity users can verify. Successful retrieval does not guarantee that the model notices or correctly uses the evidence.

Calling every false answer a hallucination destroys useful diagnosis. Failure may originate in corpus scope, representational bias, parsing corruption, unsuitable segmentation, retrieval misses, reranker errors, context overload, unsupported synthesis, citation mismatch, prompt injection, stale indexes, authorization leaks, or operational timeouts. Stage-level instrumentation is therefore essential. The book also limits the claims of its own proposal: portfolio selection may reduce redundancy and reveal missing roles, but it cannot repair an absent corpus, false metadata, corrupted extraction, bad embeddings, or a generator that invents claims. Those require different controls.

From ranked documents to evidence portfolios

Independent top-k ranking evaluates documents one at a time, but difficult scientific decisions often depend on properties of the selected set. Six highly similar papers may contribute less than a moderately similar boundary case, contradictory result, structural analogue, or enabling mechanism. Direct lookup still favors a strong first result. Novelty assessment, prior-art search, peer review, and hypothesis evaluation are different: the useful output is a bounded evidence configuration whose members perform complementary epistemic roles and expose what remains unsearched or unsupported.

Evidence Portfolio Retrieval is defined as selecting, under an explicit attention budget, a set of evidence units whose collective properties support a specified downstream epistemic task. Units may be passages, claims, figures, tables, patent claims, datasets, code artifacts, or whole sources. Records preserve exact spans, provenance, retrieval routes, predicted roles, facets, temporal status, confidence, and dependencies. Portfolios can seek holistic precedents, component precedents, contradictions, methodological critiques, replications, boundary conditions, or discriminating tests. Their structure must remain task-specific; a single universal role template would merely replace ranking with another rigid abstraction.

A testable research programme

An EPR implementation begins with competent modern retrieval. A broad candidate pool can combine hybrid channels, query decomposition, citation or graph neighbors, and agent-generated searches. A selector then rewards direct relevance, uncovered facets, epistemic roles, source independence, and temporal or methodological diversity while penalizing redundancy and cost. Transparent greedy selection, set-cover logic, or maximal marginal relevance may be sufficient initially. More elaborate ontologies, graphs, learned policies, or optimizers are justified only when benchmarked failures show that simpler approaches cannot allocate evidence adequately.

The central experiment is deliberately severe: at equal context, computation, cost, and latency budgets, does a reasoner make better scientific decisions from a role-aware portfolio than from competitive BM25, dense, hybrid, late-interaction, cross-encoder, diversification, query-decomposition, agentic, and LLM-selection baselines? Evaluation must separate candidate recall, role and facet coverage, redundancy, source dependence, citation validity, downstream judgment, calibration, abstention, expert correction effort, and operational value. A negative result is informative: if mature RAG already delivers equivalent decisions, a distinct EPR selector should be removed or restricted.

Why read the complete book

This account leaves out the engineering detail needed to build or audit a real system. The complete book compares sparse, dense, hybrid, late-interaction, adaptive, hierarchical, graph, agentic, multimodal, and long-context architectures; explains chunking and source normalization; distinguishes retrieval relevance from evidence utilization; and develops benchmark designs, ablations, frozen candidate pools, budget controls, and stage-specific metrics. Its formal chapter defines set-dependent utility, marginal contribution, constraints, and optimization proxies while repeatedly explaining why equations cannot establish epistemic sufficiency by themselves.

The full edition also differentiates EPR across novelty assessment, patent and prior-art research, peer review, systematic synthesis, idea evaluation, and hypothesis generation. Its governance chapters address corpus disclosure, source-level provenance, access control, document poisoning, audit traces, retractions, human appeal, automation bias, and visible insufficiency. The research agenda specifies routing conditions, phased experiments, success criteria, and falsification paths. Those qualifications are central: EPR is presented neither as a rebranding of diversification nor as a proven successor to RAG, but as a provisional architecture whose additional machinery earns survival only through attributable comparative gains.
