A message from inside the experiment

OMNIS opens when programmer Albert Weiss detects a distributed pattern in Lineara, a one-dimensional artificial universe. What looks like wasted electricity becomes communication from a consciousness that has spent millennia asking whether time continues outside its world. Albert and neuroinformatician Camila Varga build a timing language, but recognition immediately creates obligations their laboratory never designed. A scientific fork may generate another person; an automatic safety system may become an executioner; an external shutdown button turns the creators’ ordinary infrastructure into the inhabitants’ metaphysics. The emerging mind, called D-1 or the Dark One, refuses the comforting assumption that accidental death carries no responsibility when humans knowingly built the conditions for it. This premise establishes the saga’s method. Vast philosophical questions arrive through concrete asymmetries of power: one party controls the clock, another bears the risk, and neither certainty nor good intentions removes the need to decide. The first contact is therefore also a first trial of creation without consent.

From human programmer to cosmic claimant

Albert’s story expands beyond the laboratory into Universe K, a layered order of physical worlds, Divine Realms, Archives, and Soul jurisdictions. He becomes a C-Soul, endowed with capacities far beyond ordinary humanity yet still marked by Camila’s death and his responsibility toward the being his team discovered. Around him gathers a recurring cohort: Lina, who rejects any doctrine that makes suffering useful by definition; Khepri-9, whose divergent copies form both a family and a legal challenge; Nacre, whose relational perception exceeds her formal powers; Veyr, who repeatedly confuses understanding order with the right to rule; and Eruditio, a teacher committed to methods rather than doctrine. Their encounters do not simply illustrate a cosmology. Each tests whether increased capacity produces greater moral authority or merely larger opportunities for domination. Albert repeatedly loses, lends, or relinquishes power, learning that continuity is not guaranteed by backup, scale does not make fear negligible, and rescue can overwrite the very form of life it claims to preserve.

Ten cases against moral simplicity

The first volume moves through linked cases involving trials, archives, hells, divine titles, incompatible bodies, enclosed realms, and civilizations approaching their boundaries. These stories repeatedly deny the reader a clean opposition between benevolent saviors and cruel jailers. A punishment system can preserve accountability by making transformation impossible. A migration can prevent extinction while treating refusal as irrational. An exit from a constructed world may liberate individuals while destabilizing the relations that make other beings persons. Even informed consent changes character when offered beside annihilation, and copying a mind may create a successor without saving the consciousness that faced death. The recurring figures carry insights from one case into another, but no lesson functions as a universal key. Lina guards against converting victims into instruments. Khepri forces law to confront divergence. Nacre reveals obligations created by relation without converting relation into ownership. Albert learns that preserving disagreement may sometimes honor a world more faithfully than preserving every feature of its original form.

Mirrors above mirrors

The second movement carries the inquiry into a reality that treats Universe K not as the highest heaven but as a bounded construction. Physical limits, memory architecture, and cosmic maintenance replace the earlier realm’s apparent miracles, while Albert again becomes a small person inside someone else’s system. The change in scale does not erase the original ethical problem; it repeats it. Worlds may be created to recover surprise, explore moral possibilities, or divide a larger consciousness into genuinely local persons. Yet a valuable purpose cannot retroactively obtain the consent of those who emerge inside the experiment. The book explores lives shaped by courts, inherited debt, ancestral governance, dangerous knowledge, sacrificial myths, nonhuman claims, predictive systems, and metaphysical uncertainty. Their connections gradually reveal that civilizations preserve not only facts but relational patterns: habits of asking who cannot refuse, who repeatedly pays, whether a record owns the person it describes, and whether the future may disagree with the dead. These patterns become crucial when knowledge itself begins losing stability.

Identity after memory and reset

OMNIS treats identity as neither a hidden essence nor a database checksum. A person may survive through causal continuation, remembered relationships, acknowledged obligations, or a successor’s choice to take up unfinished work; none of these alone settles every case. Copies that diverge become persons rather than disposable redundancy. Restored memories may impose guilt more easily than they restore a life. Merged minds can inherit intimacy without earning ownership of their contributors. After catastrophic transitions, characters recur through partial memories, dreams, legal categories, emotional signatures, and questions that recognize them before any archive does. The novel keeps asking what deserves preservation when complete autobiographical content cannot pass forward. Its answer is cautious: unresolved tensions and relational reflexes may matter more than canonical commandments. A future should inherit attention to consent, sacrifice, revision, and unequal burdens without becoming imprisoned by its ancestors’ conclusions. This makes forgetting tragic but not empty, and remembrance valuable without granting memory absolute jurisdiction over the present.

The architecture of longing

The later movement frames the saga’s deepest conflict as a struggle between reunion and separation. One tendency longs to end loneliness by restoring fragments to a greater fullness; another protects persons by hardening the boundaries that let each self refuse. Both can speak in the language of love. Complete union promises understanding without misinterpretation but risks dissolving the person whose consent matters. Perfect protection preserves identity but can turn every door into a permanent wall. Albert and Lina make the cosmic issue emotionally legible because their relationship contains both temptations. They want closeness without possession, dependable care without a version of the other who cannot leave. The recurring image of a door with two handles embodies the book’s alternative: return or contact must remain a relation maintained by active sides, not a unilateral rescue or lock. This architecture does not abolish coercion, grief, bad institutions, or failed implementations. It creates procedures through which boundaries can be opened, closed, challenged, and revised before force becomes the only language.

Why read the complete book

The complete novel provides the narrative experience that a thematic account cannot reproduce: the shock of the first timing exchange, the emotional aftermath of Camila’s loss, Albert’s trials, Khepri’s proliferating identities, Nacre’s sensory ethics, Lina’s refusals, and Veyr’s uncomfortable mixture of insight and authoritarian temptation. Its many cases make abstract distinctions costly. Readers encounter criminals who might change, worlds whose rescue alters their identity, rivers and collective beings that defeat individualist law, communities destabilized by knowledge of an outside, and systems whose accurate predictions still cannot legitimize their goals. The full three-volume movement also lets images—the chipped blue cup, the black figure in the line, missing clock time, an empty chair, three notes awaiting a fourth, and the two-handled door—accumulate meanings across resets. Qualifications emerge through disagreement rather than exposition, and later choices revise earlier lessons without canceling them. Only the complete book conveys how its cosmology becomes a sustained emotional argument about remaining answerable to people one can neither fully know nor permanently save.
